A Number One in Every Depot

A delivery company ranks all of its drivers by how many packages they got out the door last month. One name lands at the top, and every other name lands somewhere underneath it. Then a manager asks a sharper question: who is the best driver at each warehouse? One flat list cannot answer that. One extra keyword can.

1. Introduction

Northline Parcel is a made-up regional package-delivery company. It picks up boxes, sorts them overnight, and gets them to homes and small businesses across one corner of the country. Northline runs three depots. A depot is just the local warehouse a driver starts and ends their shift at: they clock in there, load a van there, drive their route, and return there once the route is done. Every driver belongs to exactly one depot.

At the start of the month, the operations manager pulls a list of every driver and how many packages each one delivered. She sorts it from most to fewest and reads off the top name. That tells her who the single busiest driver in the whole company is. It does not tell her who the busiest driver at the Hillcrest warehouse is, and that is the number she actually needs, because she wants to hand out one recognition award per depot.

Look at Grace on both sides. She delivered the same number of packages either way. On the left she is fifth, buried in the middle of a list. On the right she is first, because the only drivers she is being measured against are the other drivers who load their vans at Hillcrest. Nothing about Grace changed. What changed is the group she is being compared to.

Why this matters: "best in each group" is one of the most common things a manager will ever ask an analyst for. Top-selling product in each store. Highest-paid employee in each job title. Longest wait time at each clinic. Best-performing ad in each region. It comes up so often that interviewers have a name for it and ask for it by that name, and a candidate who reaches for a separate query per group, or for a sort-and-eyeball approach, signals pretty quickly that they have not written this pattern for real.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to number rows by some value using a ranking function, and you already know how to name a finished query's result so a second query can read from it. This reading connects those two ideas. First you tell the ranking which group each row belongs to, so the numbering restarts group by group. Then you use the naming trick to hand back only the rows you actually wanted. Once that clicks, the same "compare each row to its own group" idea opens up a lot more than ranking: comparing a driver to their own depot's average, or a month to its own year's running total, works the same way.

3. Concept overview: one list, then three

Here is what the operations manager is actually working from. Northline's database has already counted up last month's deliveries per driver, so each row is one driver, their depot, and their package total.

Her first query ranks everybody against everybody:

SELECT name, depot, packages, RANK() OVER (ORDER BY packages DESC) AS rnk FROM driver_month ORDER BY rnk;

The OVER (...) part is what makes RANK() a window function: a function that looks at a set of other rows while it works out a value for the current row. The rows it is allowed to look at are called its window. Right now the window is the entire table, so Tasha gets rank 1 and everyone else falls in behind her. Grace, the fastest driver at Hillcrest, comes back as rank 5.

In that company-wide ranking, how many rows come back holding rnk = 1?

Now the manager adds two words. Everything else about the query is identical:

SELECT name, depot, packages, RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY depot ORDER BY packages DESC) AS rnk FROM driver_month ORDER BY depot, rnk;

PARTITION BY depot tells the window function to split the rows into groups by depot first, and then do its counting separately inside each group. Each of those groups is called a partition. Riverside's three drivers get numbered 1, 2, 3 among themselves. Eastgate's two drivers get numbered 1, 2 among themselves. Hillcrest's two get 1, 2. Grace goes from rank 5 to rank 1 without delivering a single extra package.

name | depot | packages | rnk ---------------+-----------+----------+----- Priya Nadar | Eastgate | 401 | 1 Luis Ferrer | Eastgate | 372 | 2 Grace Okafor | Hillcrest | 366 | 1 Sam Beckett | Hillcrest | 344 | 2 Tasha Boone | Riverside | 412 | 1 Omar Reyes | Riverside | 388 | 2 Dee Whitfield | Riverside | 355 | 3 (7 rows)

Seven rows went into that query. How many rows came back out?

The trap worth naming out loud: PARTITION BY depot and GROUP BY depot share the word "group" and do opposite things to your row count. GROUP BY depot squeezes the table down to one row per depot, and the individual driver names vanish. PARTITION BY depot never removes or merges a row. All seven drivers keep their own row, their own name, and their own package total. The only thing that changed is which other rows each rank was measured against.

7. Why you cannot just add WHERE rnk = 1

The manager has her per-depot ranking on screen. She wants the three winners and nothing else. The obvious move is to tack a filter onto the end of the query she already has:

SELECT name, depot, packages, RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY depot ORDER BY packages DESC) AS rnk FROM driver_month WHERE rnk = 1;
ERROR: column "rnk" does not exist LINE 5: WHERE rnk = 1; ^

That error is not the database being fussy about where you typed something. It is telling the literal truth. A query does not run top to bottom in the order you wrote it. The database works through it in stages, and two of those stages matter here:

  1. It gathers the rows from the table named in FROM.
  2. It throws out the rows that fail WHERE.
  3. It works out the values listed in SELECT, and that is where a window function like RANK() OVER (...) finally runs.
  4. It sorts whatever survived, using ORDER BY.

WHERE finishes at step 2. The rank is not worked out until step 3. So at the moment WHERE is deciding which rows to keep, rnk genuinely does not exist yet. There is nothing there to compare against 1.

The fix is to let the ranking finish completely, and only then filter. A CTE (common table expression) does exactly that. You wrap the ranking query in a WITH name AS (...) block, which runs it to completion and gives the finished result a name. By the time anything reads from that name, rnk is an ordinary column sitting there like depot or packages, and an outer WHERE can filter on it with no trouble at all.

WITH ranked AS ( SELECT name, depot, packages, RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY depot ORDER BY packages DESC) AS rnk FROM driver_month ) SELECT name, depot, packages FROM ranked WHERE rnk = 1 ORDER BY depot;
name | depot | packages ---------------+-----------+---------- Priya Nadar | Eastgate | 401 Grace Okafor | Hillcrest | 366 Tasha Boone | Riverside | 412 (3 rows)

Three rows, one award per depot. This shape has a name that interviewers use out loud: top-N per group. Rank inside each group, then keep the top rows of each group. Change rnk = 1 to rnk <= 3 and you have a top-three-per-depot report from the exact same query.

Why does WHERE rnk = 1 fail in the first version of that query?

A note on HAVING: if you learned HAVING as "the WHERE for grouped results," you may be tempted to try HAVING rnk = 1 next. That fails for the same reason. HAVING also runs before the SELECT list is worked out, so the rank does not exist yet there either. The CTE wrapper is the fix in both cases.

8. Build it in the right order

Referring to the winners-only query above, click the steps in the order you would actually write it, then self-check.

  • Write the ranking query on its own and look at its output to confirm the numbers restart per depot.
  • Wrap that whole query in a WITH ranked AS (...) block so its result has a name.
  • Write an outer query that selects from ranked.
  • Add WHERE rnk = 1 to that outer query.

This matters just as much when an AI assistant writes the query. Asking an AI tool for a "best driver per depot" query will usually get you something that looks right, and a wrong grouping column is almost invisible on a large result. The check is cheap: pick one depot, count its drivers by hand from the raw table, and confirm the query named the same winner you did. Do that before you send the numbers to anyone.

11. How this shows up in interviews

Interviewers ask for this pattern constantly, usually phrased as "the top two highest-paid employees in each department," "the best-selling product in each category," or "the most recent order for each customer." They are all the same question wearing different clothes, and the phrase for it is top-N per group.

What separates a strong answer is saying the plan out loud before writing anything. Something like: "I would rank inside each department with PARTITION BY department ORDER BY salary DESC, put that in a CTE, then filter the outer query on the rank, because a window function is not available to WHERE in the same query." Naming why the wrapper is needed is what shows you have actually run into that error rather than memorized a template. If the interviewer asks about a ranking function you have never used, say so honestly and reason out loud from the ones you have used. That reads far better than a confident guess.

This is not only a data-analyst question. Backend and full-stack engineering interviews ask for the same shape any time a feature needs "the latest row per user" or "the top comment per post," and the same CTE wrapper is the answer there too.

Interview-style question: an interviewer asks for the two highest-paid employees in each job title, from one employees table. Which plan gets there?

12. Summary & critical thinking

Two ideas carried this whole reading. First, PARTITION BY inside an OVER clause decides which other rows a ranking is measured against, and the ranking restarts inside each of those groups while every row stays exactly where it was. Second, a window function is worked out after WHERE has already finished, so filtering on a rank means finishing the ranking inside a CTE and putting the filter in the outer query.

New situation: a coffee chain has one table with one row per drink sold per store, already totaled up as store, drink, and cups_sold. The owner wants the single best-selling drink at each store, by name. Which approach gets there?

13. Thinking ahead

Ranking is only the first thing a window function can do with a partition. The same PARTITION BY idea attaches to other functions too. Ask for an average over a partition and every driver's row can carry their own depot's average beside their personal total, so you can see at a glance who is above or below their local pace. Ask for a running total over a partition ordered by date and each row can carry the year-to-date figure for its own group. In every one of those cases the question you are really answering is the same one from this reading: compared to whom? Getting comfortable naming the right partition column is most of the work, and it carries straight over.
