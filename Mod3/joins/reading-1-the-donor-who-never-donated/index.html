The Donor Who Never Donated

A small nonprofit keeps a list of every supporter who has ever signed up to hear from them, and a separate list of every donation anyone has actually made. Most supporters show up on both lists — but one shows up on only one of them. Whether that supporter disappears from your results or stays visible with a gap depends entirely on one word in your query: INNER or LEFT.

1. Introduction

Bright Path Literacy is a small nonprofit with two spreadsheets that never quite talk to each other. One lists every supporter who has ever signed up for their mailing list. The other lists every donation anyone has actually made. Most supporters appear in both. One supporter — Jo Alvarez, who signed up six months ago and has been meaning to give — appears in the first list and nowhere in the second.

The development coordinator asks two very different questions of the exact same two tables: "Show me everyone who has actually donated, and how much," and separately, "Show me every single supporter we have, whether they've given yet or not, so I know who to follow up with." Both questions require combining the two tables. They do not require the same combination.

Notice the dashed lines: they connect Priya's row and Marcus's row across both tables, because their id and supporter_id values match. There's no line to Jo, because nothing in donations matches her id of 103. The INNER JOIN result only keeps rows where that connection exists, so Jo disappears entirely. The LEFT JOIN result keeps every row from supporters regardless, and simply fills in NULL wherever no match was found.

Why this matters: "which of our INNER JOIN and LEFT JOIN would return the same result if the data always matched perfectly?" is a trap. Real data almost never matches perfectly — new supporters who haven't given yet, orders with a missing shipping record, employees who haven't finished onboarding. Picking the wrong join type doesn't crash your program or throw an error. It just silently produces a report that's missing exactly the people or records you most needed to see. That's a real, recurring risk in any job that touches a relational database, and it's one of the single most commonly asked SQL interview questions at every level.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to write a SELECT against one table, filter it with WHERE, and sort it with ORDER BY. This reading is about the next move: pulling matching rows from two related tables into a single result set, and deciding on purpose whether "no match" should mean "leave it out" or "show it anyway, with a gap." Once that distinction is solid, summarizing what a join returns — counting, grouping, totaling — builds directly on top of it.

3. Concept overview: one query, two very different answers

Both of Bright Path's tables exist independently. supporters has one row per person on the mailing list. donations has one row per gift, and only people who've actually given appear in it at all. To answer either of the coordinator's questions, both tables have to be combined in a single query using a join — and the ON clause that connects them is called the join condition.

SELECT s.name, d.amount FROM supporters AS s INNER JOIN donations AS d ON s.id = d.supporter_id;

INNER JOIN only keeps a row when the join condition finds a real match on both sides. Priya matches, Marcus matches, Jo doesn't — so Jo's row from supporters simply never makes it into the result. This is also what a plain JOIN means by default in SQL: unqualified JOIN and INNER JOIN are the exact same thing.

SELECT s.name, d.amount FROM supporters AS s LEFT JOIN donations AS d ON s.id = d.supporter_id;

Change one word — LEFT instead of INNER — and the meaning flips. LEFT JOIN keeps every row from the table named first (the "left" table, supporters), whether or not the join condition finds a match. When there's no match, every column that would have come from the right-hand table (donations) is filled in with NULL instead of being left out. Jo stays in the result, with NULL sitting where her donation amount would be.

A related trap: once you have a result with a NULL in it, you might try WHERE amount = NULL to find those rows. That comparison silently returns nothing, always — NULL means "unknown/absent," and SQL never considers an unknown value equal to anything, even another unknown value. The only correct way to check is WHERE amount IS NULL.

9. Debugging a join that returns fewer rows than expected

You expected every supporter to show up in a report and only some did. Click the steps below, in the order you'd actually check them, then self-check.

  • Confirm which join keyword you actually used — INNER or LEFT.
  • Re-read the join condition for a typo or a wrong column.
  • Pick one row you expected to see and check it by hand against the raw source tables.
  • Compare the total row count to what you'd predict from a small, known sample.

This applies even when an AI assistant wrote the query. Asking an AI tool to draft a join is fine — trusting its output without checking it isn't. The same habit practiced above (picking one row you know the right answer for, and checking it by hand against the source tables) is exactly how you'd verify an AI-written join actually did what you asked before you rely on it for anything real.

11. How this shows up in interviews

"What's the difference between an INNER JOIN and a LEFT JOIN, and when would you use each?" is one of the single most commonly asked SQL questions in data interviews, at every level from first internship to senior analyst. Interviewers ask it because it's a fast, reliable way to tell whether a candidate has actually written real joins against real data, or only memorized the definitions.

A strong answer names the mechanical difference (matched-only vs. every-left-row-plus-NULL) and gives a real reason to choose one over the other — "I'd use LEFT JOIN here because I specifically need to find the supporters who haven't donated yet, and INNER JOIN would hide exactly those rows." Naming a real use case is what separates a memorized definition from a demonstrated skill.

Interview-style question: you LEFT JOIN a customers table (1,000 rows) with an orders table on customer id, and get back 1,000 rows. The equivalent INNER JOIN on the same tables returns only 640 rows. What's the most likely explanation?

12. Summary & critical thinking

Two ideas carried this whole reading: a join needs a condition connecting two tables on matching values, and the join type you pick decides what happens to a row on one side that has no match at all — INNER JOIN drops it, LEFT JOIN keeps it and fills the gap with NULL.

New situation: a food delivery app has a drivers table and a deliveries table. Management wants a list of every driver who has NEVER completed a single delivery, so they can follow up. Which approach gets there?

13. Thinking ahead

Section 6's library scenario had Chen show up twice in the joined result, because she genuinely had two checkouts. That's correct and expected, but it also means counting rows straight out of a join doesn't always tell you what you think it does. The moment you start summarizing a joined result (how many books did each member check out, how much has each supporter given in total), you have to account for the fact that a join can multiply rows for anything with more than one match on the "many" side. Holding that in mind now makes the next skill, collapsing those rows back down per real-world entity before counting or totaling them, much easier to reason about.
