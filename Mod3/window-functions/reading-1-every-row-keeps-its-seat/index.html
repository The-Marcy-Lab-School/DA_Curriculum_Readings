Every Row Keeps Its Seat

A small bank runs the same four numbers through two different queries. One query hands back a tidy summary and quietly deletes every person behind it. The other hands back all four people, each with a placement written beside their name. Same data, same database, two very different answers.

1. Introduction

Riverbend Community Credit Union is a small bank in a mid-sized town. A credit union is a bank that is owned by the people who bank there, not by outside investors. Members elect the board. Profits come back to members as better rates instead of going to shareholders.

Riverbend has two branches and four loan officers. A loan officer is the person who walks a member through applying for a car loan, a home loan, or a small business loan, and gets it approved and signed. "Closing" a loan means finishing that whole process. Last quarter, the four officers closed these numbers of loans:

officer_quarter
officer_namebranchloans
Dario VegaRiverside31
Nia OkaforRiverside26
Sam WhitfieldEastgate26
Priya RamanEastgate19

The branch manager wants a short report for the quarterly member meeting. She wants every officer listed by name, in order, with a placement beside each one, so she can read the whole list out loud and thank people. She also wants the two officers who tied at 26 to be shown as tied, because they genuinely are.

If you reach for GROUP BY here, you will get a summary and lose the people. Here is what each approach actually returns.

Look at the two results side by side. The gold one on the left ran GROUP BY branch. It added the loans up per branch and returned two rows. The totals are correct, but Dario, Nia, Sam and Priya are no longer anywhere in the result. Their rows were folded into the totals and then dropped.

The blue one on the right ran a window function. That is a calculation that looks across a set of rows and writes one value onto each row, without folding any rows together. All four officers are still there. A new column named rnk now holds each one's placement. Notice that Nia and Sam both show 2, because they really did tie, and Priya shows 4 rather than 3.

Why this matters: "give me the top 5 sales reps," "flag each customer's most recent order," "show me where every store ranks" are ordinary requests in an analyst job, and none of them can be answered by collapsing rows into a summary. The person asking wants the individual rows back, labeled. Choosing GROUP BY here does not throw an error. It just hands back a report that answers a question nobody asked. Interviewers lean on this because it separates people who have written real reports from people who have only memorized aggregate syntax.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to sort rows with ORDER BY and how to summarize them with GROUP BY and a count. This reading adds a third move that sits between those two: keep every row exactly as it is, and attach a computed number to each one. Once that idea is solid, the same clause extends to ranking inside groups, running totals, and comparing a row against the row before it.

Three skills are prerequisites for this reading: sorting with ORDER BY, summarizing with GROUP BY plus an aggregate function, and renaming a result column with AS. Test each one before moving on.

A window function leans on all three of these. It uses an ORDER BY of its own to decide what "first" means, it is deliberately not a GROUP BY, and its result almost always gets an AS name so you can refer to the new column.

3. Concept overview: two queries, the same four numbers

Here is the table again, so you do not have to scroll back up.

officer_quarter
officer_namebranchloans
Dario VegaRiverside31
Nia OkaforRiverside26
Sam WhitfieldEastgate26
Priya RamanEastgate19

First, the summary the branch manager did not ask for:

SELECT branch, SUM(loans) AS total_loans FROM officer_quarter GROUP BY branch;

GROUP BY branch gathers every row that shares a branch value, adds the loans column up across that group, and emits one row for the whole group. The four original rows are consumed in the process. There is no way to ask that result who Dario is, because Dario's row no longer exists in it.

Now the report she actually wanted:

SELECT officer_name, loans, RANK() OVER (ORDER BY loans DESC) AS rnk FROM officer_quarter;

There is no GROUP BY in that query at all. Every row from officer_quarter comes out the other side untouched, with one extra column bolted on. The new piece is OVER (ORDER BY loans DESC). That is the OVER clause, and it tells RANK() which rows to look across and in what order. Without it, RANK() would have no idea what "first place" even means.

The one-sentence version worth memorizing: GROUP BY replaces rows with a summary. A window function leaves the rows alone and writes a value onto each of them.

5. Write the clause yourself

A window function is always two pieces: the function name with empty parentheses, then the OVER (...) part that defines what it looks across. Here is the query with one keyword removed.

SELECT officer_name, loans, RANK() ______ (ORDER BY loans DESC) AS rnk FROM officer_quarter;
Need a hint?

It is a single short word. It sits between the function's empty parentheses and the parentheses holding the ORDER BY. Section 3 named it out loud.

Two details are easy to trip on, so read them slowly.

  1. The parentheses right after RANK stay empty. Nothing goes inside them.
  2. The ORDER BY inside OVER (...) is a separate thing from a query's own final ORDER BY. This one decides how rows get numbered. A query can have both, and they do not have to match.

6. The tie, and two honest answers to it

Nia and Sam both closed 26 loans. That is a real tie, not a data error. RANK() and ROW_NUMBER() handle it differently, and neither one is wrong.

RANK() OVER (ORDER BY loans DESC)
officer_nameloansrnk
Dario Vega311
Nia Okafor262
Sam Whitfield262
Priya Raman194
ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY loans DESC)
officer_nameloansrn
Dario Vega311
Nia Okafor262
Sam Whitfield263
Priya Raman194

RANK() reports the tie as a tie: 1, 2, 2, 4. Two officers share second place, so third place is skipped and the next distinct value lands on 4. That skip is not a bug. Two people already occupied the second and third slots, so the fourth slot is genuinely the next one free.

ROW_NUMBER() refuses to repeat a number: 1, 2, 3, 4. Sam gets 3 purely because the database had to put the two tied rows in some order. Nothing in the data says Nia beat Sam. ROW_NUMBER() made that call anyway, because it was asked to.

Under RANK(), what number does Priya Raman get?

Under ROW_NUMBER() instead, what number does Sam Whitfield get?

The real decision: a tie in the data is not a problem to fix. It means somebody has to choose a tie-breaking rule on purpose. RANK() says "these two are equal, and I will say so." ROW_NUMBER() says "I will put them in some order because you need a clean list." Pick based on what the report has to promise, and say which one you picked and why.

8. What the database does under the hood

Click the steps below in the order the database actually carries them out for RANK() OVER (ORDER BY loans DESC), then self-check.

  • Collect the rows the query already selected, all four of them.
  • Put those rows in order using the ORDER BY written inside the OVER clause.
  • Walk the sorted rows and assign each one a number using the function's tie rule.
  • Hand back every original row, now carrying its number in a new column.

This matters when an AI assistant writes the query for you. Asking an AI tool to draft a ranking query is fine. Trusting it without checking is not. AI tools mix up RANK() and ROW_NUMBER() often, because the difference only shows up on rows that tie, and most sample data has no ties in it. Verify it the way you just walked through: pick a tie you already know exists, and check by hand what number each side of it got.

11. How this shows up in interviews

"Explain the difference between GROUP BY and a window function" is asked constantly in data interviews, and so is the follow-up: "RANK() or ROW_NUMBER(), and why?" A strong answer names the mechanism, not just the syntax. GROUP BY collapses rows into one per group. A window function keeps all of them and adds a value to each.

The version that stands out goes one step further and asks a clarifying question back. "Top 3" is ambiguous the moment a tie is possible. An interviewer who hears you notice that before you start typing has learned more about you than any correct query would show.

Interview-style question: your manager asks for "the top 3 loan officers this quarter" for a slide that has exactly three lines on it. Two officers are tied at third. What is the strongest response?

12. Summary and critical thinking

Two ideas carried this whole reading. First, a window function does not collapse anything: the rows you hand it are the rows you get back, each with a computed value attached. Second, RANK() and ROW_NUMBER() only disagree on rows that tie, and choosing between them is a decision about what the report has to promise, not a matter of one being more correct.

New situation: a food co-op grocery ranks its six delivery drivers by on-time deliveries last month. The report must list all six drivers by name, and two drivers with identical counts must show the same placement. Which approach delivers that?

13. Thinking ahead

Every query in this reading ranked all four officers against each other, across the whole table. Riverbend has two branches, though, and the branch manager will eventually want a separate first place inside each branch rather than one list for the whole credit union. The same OVER (...) clause handles that. It takes one more instruction that says "restart the numbering for each branch," and the function name and the ORDER BY stay exactly as they are. The same clause also carries running totals and row-to-row comparisons, so the syntax you just typed is the foundation for a whole family of calculations, not a single trick.
