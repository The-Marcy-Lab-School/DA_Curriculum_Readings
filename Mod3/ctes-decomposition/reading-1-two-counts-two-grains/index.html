Two Counts, Two Grains

A video site shows each channel a short report: how many videos it posted, and how many comments those videos got. One query that joins three tables answers it in six lines. The comment counts are right. The video counts are too big. Nothing gives an error.

3. A join copies rows

The grain of a table is what one row stands for. The grain of videos is one video. The grain of comments is one comment. Here is the direct attempt at the report: join all three tables, group by channel, and count both things.

Predict: what video_count does flat_report.sql show for Plant Pals?

 channel_name | video_count | comment_count
--------------+-------------+---------------
 Plant Pals   |           3 |             3
 Bike Fix     |           1 |             0
 Star Maps    |           2 |             2
(3 rows)

Plant Pals has 2 videos, not 3. To see why, look at the rows the joins build before any counting happens:

 channel_name | video_id | comment_id
--------------+----------+------------
 Plant Pals   |      101 |       9001
 Plant Pals   |      101 |       9002
 Plant Pals   |      102 |       9003
 Bike Fix     |      103 |
 Star Maps    |      104 |       9004
 Star Maps    |      104 |       9005
(6 rows)

Video 101 has two comments, so the join writes video 101 out twice. That copying is called row multiplication, or fan-out: when one row matches several rows in the next table, it gets copied once per match. The join did its job. The mistake is counting videos in a result whose grain is one comment. The rule: counting two things at two different grains in one flat join makes the bigger-grain count too big.

Star Maps has 1 video, but flat_report.sql showed a video_count of 2. Why?

4. Count the column, not the row

Look at Bike Fix in the joined rows. Its video 103 has no comments, but a LEFT JOIN still keeps one row for it, with NULL (no value) in comment_id. Two ways of counting disagree on that row. COUNT(*) counts every row in the group, whatever is in it. COUNT(column) counts only the rows where that column has a real value, and skips the NULLs.

Predict: in count_check.sql, what number goes in the rows_counted column for video 103?

 video_id | rows_counted | real_comments
----------+--------------+---------------
      103 |            1 |             0
(1 row)

The habit: after a LEFT JOIN, count the column you care about. COUNT(c.comment_id) answers "how many comments." COUNT(*) answers "how many rows came out of the join."

5. Plan the steps, then chain them

A decomposition plan is a short, step-by-step sketch of how you will answer a question, written in plain sentences before any SQL. Each step becomes one named result. For the channel report:

  1. Count the comments on each video. (This is the smallest grain, so it goes first.)
  2. Roll that up to each channel: count the videos and add up their comment counts.
  3. Check one channel against a count you did by hand.

Predict: comments_per_video.sql is step 1 on its own. What does one row of its result stand for?

 video_id | channel_id | comment_count
----------+------------+---------------
      101 |          1 |             2
      102 |          1 |             1
      103 |          2 |             0
      104 |          3 |             2
(4 rows)

Four rows, one per video, and nothing was copied. You can check this middle step before building on it. Now step 2. A chained CTE is two or more named results in one WITH clause, separated by a comma, where a later one uses an earlier one by name. The word WITH appears only once.

Fill in the blanks: chain step 2 onto step 1

WITH comments_per_video AS ( SELECT v.video_id, v.channel_id, COUNT(c.comment_id) AS comment_count FROM videos v LEFT JOIN comments c ON c.video_id = v.video_id GROUP BY v.video_id, v.channel_id )____(1) channel_totals AS ( SELECT ch.channel_id, ch.channel_name, COUNT(cv.video_id) AS video_count, SUM(cv.comment_count) AS comment_count FROM channels ch LEFT JOIN ____(2) cv ON cv.channel_id = ch.channel_id GROUP BY ch.channel_id, ch.channel_name ) SELECT channel_name, video_count, comment_count FROM channel_totals ORDER BY channel_id;
  1. In box (1), type the one punctuation mark that separates two named results inside one WITH clause. Just the mark, nothing else.
  2. In box (2), type the name of step 1, spelled exactly as it is after WITH. Step 2 joins to step 1's result, which has one row per video, so nothing gets copied.
  3. Press Check my chain. The page tells you which blank, if any, needs another look.

7. Wrap-up

  • A join copies rows, so counting two things at two different grains in one flat join makes one count too big.
  • After a LEFT JOIN, COUNT(column) gives the honest count; COUNT(*) counts rows.
  • A chained CTE handles the smallest grain first, then rolls it up. A comma separates the steps.

New situation: the site adds a fourth table, replies, with one row per reply to a comment. The report now needs, for each channel, the number of comments and the number of replies. What should the first named step compute?

In interviews: "How would you answer a question that needs three or more tables?" is common. Say your plan out loud and name the overcounting risk before you write any SQL.

Where this goes next: a chain can keep going, with a third or fourth named step, each one using the steps defined before it.
