Two Counts, Two Grains

A clinic asks for one simple report: for each patient, how many visits, and how many prescriptions. One query joining all three tables answers it in about six lines, and the numbers it hands back are wrong. Nothing errors out. The count is just quietly too big.

1. Introduction

Fern Hollow Community Health is a small neighborhood clinic. A community health clinic is a clinic that sees patients regardless of whether they can pay. Some patients have insurance, some do not, and nobody is turned away. Clinics like this run on grants, and grant paperwork asks for numbers.

The clinic manager needs one report: for each patient, how many visits have they had, and how many prescriptions were written across all of those visits? A prescription here just means one medication a provider wrote for a patient during a visit. One visit can produce two prescriptions, one, or none at all.

The clinic's database keeps that in three tables. Each one means something different by "a row."

Read those three tables against each other for a second. Maya is one person. She has two visits. Across those two visits, three prescriptions were written. One patient, two visits, three prescriptions. Three different counts of the same person, and all three are correct, because each table is counting a different thing.

The picture on the right is the whole problem in one image. Joining all three tables at once flattens them into a single wide result, and Maya's two visits get copied out to three rows, one for each prescription. Ask that flattened result how many visits Maya had and it answers honestly: it can see three visit values, so it says 3.

Why this matters: a wrong number that looks reasonable is worse than an error message. A grant report saying a clinic delivered 3,400 visits when it delivered 2,100 is a real problem for real funding, and nothing in the query flags it. This exact mistake, counting two things at two different levels in one flat join, is one of the most common bugs in analyst work, and "how would you approach a question that needs three or more tables?" is a standard question in data interviews precisely because of it.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to join two tables, how to count with GROUP BY, and how to name one intermediate result with a WITH clause. This reading puts a second named result after the first one, separated by a comma, so the second can use what the first produced. Once that clicks, the same pattern keeps going: three named steps, four, each one a small query you can run and check on its own before the next one leans on it.

3. Concept overview: the six-line query that lies

Here is the direct attempt. Join all three tables, group by patient, count both things.

SELECT pt.first_name, COUNT(v.visit_id) AS visit_count, COUNT(p.rx_id) AS rx_total FROM patients pt LEFT JOIN visits v ON v.patient_id = pt.patient_id LEFT JOIN prescriptions p ON p.visit_id = v.visit_id GROUP BY pt.patient_id, pt.first_name;

That query runs. It returns three rows, one per patient, with two numbers each. It looks like a finished report.

Maya's visit count came back as 3 when she had 2. Rosa's came back as 2 when she had 1. The prescription totals are all fine.

Here is why. The two LEFT JOINs build one wide, flat result before any counting happens, and a patient gets one row in that flat result for every prescription, not for every visit. Maya's visit 101 had two prescriptions, so visit 101 gets written out twice. Her visit 102 had one, so it gets written out once. Three rows, and her visit number appears three times.

This copying is called row multiplication, or fan-out. It is not a bug in the database. The join did exactly what a join does. The mistake is asking that one flat result for two counts that live at two different levels.

The prescription totals survived because prescriptions are the finest level here. Every prescription is on exactly one row, so counting them is honest. The visit count is the one that got multiplied, because visits sit one level above prescriptions.

The one-sentence version worth memorizing: a join copies rows, so counting two things at two different levels in one flat join will inflate the coarser one.

6. Darnell's visit, and the COUNT(*) trap

Look back at Darnell. He has one visit, number 103, and no prescriptions were written at it. Under a LEFT JOIN, his visit still produces a row, and the prescription columns on that row are empty.

Darnell's single flat row
first_namevisit_idrx_id
Darnell103(empty)

That empty cell is where COUNT(*) and COUNT(rx_id) stop agreeing. COUNT(*) counts rows. There is one row, so it says 1. COUNT(rx_id) counts real values in the rx_id column. There are none, so it says 0. Zero is the true answer.

For Darnell's visit 103, what does COUNT(*) report as the number of prescriptions?

The habit to build: after a LEFT JOIN, count the column you actually care about, not the row. COUNT(rx_id) answers "how many prescriptions." COUNT(*) answers "how many rows came out of my join," which is a different question that happens to give the same answer only when nothing is missing.

7. Sketch the plan before the SQL

Before writing a query that spans three tables, write the plan in plain sentences. Naming the steps in English first is what keeps you from mashing everything into one join and hoping.

Click the steps below in the order a working plan puts them, then self-check. Click a step again to remove it if you change your mind.

  • Write down the real question in one sentence, and notice it asks for two counts at two different levels.
  • Handle the deepest level first: count prescriptions for each single visit.
  • Roll that per-visit result up to each patient, counting visits and adding the prescription counts together.
  • Check the finished numbers against one patient you counted by hand.

That plan has two computing steps in it, which is the real signal. Two steps in the plan means two named results in the query.

8. Step one, on its own

The first named result answers only the first plan step: for each visit, how many prescriptions.

WITH rx_per_visit AS ( SELECT v.visit_id, v.patient_id, COUNT(p.rx_id) AS rx_count FROM visits v LEFT JOIN prescriptions p ON p.visit_id = v.visit_id GROUP BY v.visit_id, v.patient_id ) SELECT * FROM rx_per_visit ORDER BY visit_id;

Two tables, one join, one count. Nothing here is new. And because it is a complete query on its own, you can run it and look at the output before writing anything else:

Four rows, one per visit. The grain is exactly one appointment per row, which is the grain the original visits table had. Nothing multiplied. Visit 103 shows 0, not 1, because the count is COUNT(p.rx_id) and not COUNT(*).

That checkable middle step is the practical argument for chaining. You are not staring at one dense query wondering which half is wrong. You looked at four rows and confirmed they were right.

What is the grain of rx_per_visit, meaning what does one of its rows represent?

9. Step two, chained onto step one

The second named result uses the first one by name, exactly as if rx_per_visit were a real table sitting in the database. The piece that makes this legal is small: a comma after the closing parenthesis of the first definition.

WITH rx_per_visit AS ( SELECT v.visit_id, v.patient_id, COUNT(p.rx_id) AS rx_count FROM visits v LEFT JOIN prescriptions p ON p.visit_id = v.visit_id GROUP BY v.visit_id, v.patient_id ), patient_totals AS ( SELECT pt.patient_id, pt.first_name, COUNT(r.visit_id) AS visit_count, SUM(r.rx_count) AS rx_total FROM patients pt LEFT JOIN rx_per_visit r ON r.patient_id = pt.patient_id GROUP BY pt.patient_id, pt.first_name ) SELECT first_name, visit_count, rx_total FROM patient_totals ORDER BY rx_total DESC;

Notice what the second step is joining to. Not visits, and not prescriptions. It joins patients to rx_per_visit, which already holds exactly one row per visit with the prescription count folded into it. There is nothing left to multiply. COUNT(r.visit_id) counts visits, SUM(r.rx_count) adds up the per-visit counts, and both come out right.

Final result
first_namevisit_countrx_total
Maya23
Rosa12
Darnell10

Two visits and three prescriptions for Maya. One visit and two prescriptions for Rosa. One visit and zero prescriptions for Darnell. Those match the hand count from the start of this reading.

One small detail worth copying: both GROUP BY clauses include the id, not just the name. Grouping by first_name alone would quietly merge two different patients who happen to share a first name into one row.

Now write the chaining yourself. Here is the same query with two pieces removed.

WITH rx_per_visit AS ( SELECT v.visit_id, v.patient_id, COUNT(p.rx_id) AS rx_count FROM visits v LEFT JOIN prescriptions p ON p.visit_id = v.visit_id GROUP BY v.visit_id, v.patient_id )___ patient_totals AS ( SELECT pt.patient_id, pt.first_name, COUNT(r.visit_id) AS visit_count, SUM(r.rx_count) AS rx_total FROM patients pt LEFT JOIN __________ r ON r.patient_id = pt.patient_id GROUP BY pt.patient_id, pt.first_name ) SELECT * FROM patient_totals;
Need a hint?

Blank 1 is the single punctuation mark that separates two definitions inside one WITH clause. The word WITH is never repeated. Blank 2 is the name the first definition was given a few lines above, being used as if it were a table.

12. Could one big named result have worked?

Honest answer: sometimes. COUNT(DISTINCT v.visit_id) would have fixed the visit count in the flat query from section 3. DISTINCT means "ignore repeats," so counting distinct visit ids gives 2 for Maya even though her visit id appears three times.

So why not just do that every time? Two reasons.

  1. It only rescues counts. The moment the report needs a total instead of a count, say adding up a dollar amount or a supply quantity per visit, the copied rows get added together and DISTINCT cannot undo that.
  2. It gives you nothing to check. A single dense query is right or wrong as a whole. A chain lets you run the first step, look at four rows, confirm they are correct, and only then build on them.

Chaining is not a style preference here. It is what keeps each aggregation honest before the next one runs.

This matters when an AI assistant writes the query for you. Asking an AI tool for a three-table report is a reasonable thing to do. Trusting the output without checking is not. AI tools produce the flat all-at-once join constantly, because it looks like the obvious shape of the question, and the result comes back with plausible numbers and no error. Verify it the way you just did by hand: pick one patient whose real numbers you already know, run the query, and compare.

A report needs each patient's visit count and the total dollars billed across all their visits, where billed amounts live on the visits table. The flat all-at-once join is used. Which number breaks, and can DISTINCT save it?

14. How this shows up in interviews

"How would you approach a business question that needs data from three or more tables?" is a standard question in data interviews, and it is testing your process, not your syntax recall. A strong answer walks through it in order: restate the question, plan the steps in plain language, name what each step computes, write one named result per step, then validate the final numbers against a small sample counted by hand.

The detail that makes an answer stand out is naming the overcounting risk before anyone asks about it. Saying "these two counts are at different levels, so I would compute the deeper one first rather than joining everything at once" shows you have written reports that were wrong once and learned from it.

Interview-style question: an interviewer asks for each store's number of orders and the total number of items across those orders, from three tables: stores, orders, and order_items. What is the strongest opening move?

15. Summary and critical thinking

Two ideas carried this reading. First, a join copies rows, so two counts taken at two different levels in one flat join will inflate the coarser one, silently and without any error. Second, a comma inside a WITH clause lets you name a second step that uses the first, which both fixes the inflation and gives you a checkpoint you can verify on its own before moving on.

New situation: a public library wants, for each library card holder, how many times they borrowed anything and how many individual items they took home in total. The data lives in members, checkouts (one row per trip to the desk), and checkout_items (one row per book or DVD in that trip). What should the first named step compute?

16. Thinking ahead

This reading chained exactly two named steps. Nothing stops a third. A question like "which patients had more prescriptions than the clinic average" adds one more comma and one more name: count per visit, roll up per patient, then compare each patient against the average of those totals. The rule stays the same the whole way down. Each name can use the names defined before it, and none can use a name defined after it, so the chain reads straight down the page in the order the work actually happens.

The other thing that travels forward is the habit of asking what one row means before writing a join. That question comes back every time a report mixes counts, totals, and averages from tables that sit at different levels.
