Clearing the Bar

A three-person design collective called Bright Path keeps one long table of every project it has ever billed: which client hired them, what kind of project it was, how many hours it took, and what the invoice came to. The founders want two different answers out of that same table: which clients keep coming back, and which of those repeat clients are actually worth prioritizing because their total business clears a real revenue bar. The first question means collapsing many project rows into one row per client. The second means throwing out some of those collapsed rows, based on a number that doesn't even exist until the collapsing already happened. This reading is about both moves.

1. Concept overview

Here's a slice of Bright Path's projects table, six rows in:

client_name project_type hours_billed invoice_amount year Harbor Realty Group logo design 10 1200 2025 Pinewood Dental website design 26 3100 2025 Union Grain Bakery brand refresh 22 2600 2025 Harbor Realty Group packaging design 14 1700 2026 Union Grain Bakery packaging design 18 2200 2026 Union Grain Bakery logo design 9 1100 2025

Three real clients, six rows, in whatever order the projects actually shipped. "How much total revenue has each client brought in?" can't be answered by reading any single row: the answer for Union Grain Bakery is scattered across rows 3, 5, and 6. GROUP BY is SQL's answer. It gathers every row that shares the same value in one column, one client here, into its own group, runs an aggregate function separately inside each group, and returns exactly one output row per group:

SELECT client_name, SUM(invoice_amount) AS total_revenue FROM projects GROUP BY client_name; [Output] client_name total_revenue Harbor Realty Group 2900 Pinewood Dental 3100 Union Grain Bakery 5900

Now the founders ask the second question: "which of our repeat clients are worth prioritizing?" Repeat means COUNT(*) > 1, a number that only exists after grouping happens. WHERE can't help here: WHERE throws out individual rows before any grouping ever starts, so at the moment it runs, there's no group and no count for it to check yet. HAVING is the clause built for exactly this: it runs after the groups are already formed and the aggregate already calculated, and it decides which whole groups survive into the final result.

SELECT client_name, COUNT(*) AS project_count FROM projects GROUP BY client_name HAVING COUNT(*) > 1; [Output] client_name project_count Harbor Realty Group 2 Union Grain Bakery 3

Why this matters: "which of these clients/customers/accounts actually clears some threshold" is one of the most common real questions in a data role, whether the threshold is "more than 3 orders," "over $10,000 in total spend," or "at least 5 support tickets this month." GROUP BY plus HAVING is the two-step pattern behind almost every one of those questions, and mixing up which clause does which job is one of the most common, most visible SQL mistakes there is.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to join tables and filter individual rows with WHERE. This reading is about summarizing many rows into one row per real-world thing, and then filtering on a number that only exists after that summarizing happens. Once both clauses feel automatic, the natural next step is wrapping a query like this one inside a name of its own, so a bigger question can build on top of it without repeating the whole thing.

4. Write the query: repeat clients only

The founders want each client's name and how many projects they've hired Bright Path for, but only for clients who have hired them more than once. Fill in the two missing clauses:

SELECT client_name, COUNT(*) AS project_count
FROM projects
client_name
COUNT(*) > 1;
Need a hint?

The first blank has to run before COUNT(*) can even exist. The second blank has to run after it, since it tests the result of COUNT(*) directly.

7. Order the steps: how a grouped, filtered query actually runs

Click steps, in order, to build the sequence this query actually follows when it runs, not the order it's typed in:

SELECT client_name, COUNT(*) AS project_count FROM projects WHERE year = 2025 GROUP BY client_name HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 ORDER BY project_count DESC;

Click a step again to remove it if you change your mind, then press "Check my order" when ready. This is a self-check, not graded on correctness.

  • FROM identifies which table the rows come from.
  • WHERE filters out individual rows, before any grouping happens.
  • GROUP BY gathers the surviving rows into groups sharing the same client_name.
  • HAVING filters out whole groups, based on the now-computed COUNT(*).
  • SELECT decides which columns and expressions appear in the output.
  • ORDER BY sorts the final rows for display.

10. How this shows up in interviews

"Find all customers who have placed more than 3 orders" (or its cousins: accounts over a spend threshold, users with more than N logins) is one of the most common take-home and live-coding SQL prompts in a data-role interview. It's a GROUP BY plus HAVING query almost every time, and interviewers are listening for whether you reach for HAVING immediately, or fumble through a WHERE clause first and have to self-correct.

That self-correction moment is worth naming: writing WHERE COUNT(*) > 3 by instinct and then catching yourself is one of the most common, most visible mistakes in a live SQL interview. Catching it out loud, "wait, that has to be HAVING, since COUNT(*) doesn't exist yet at the WHERE stage," reads as real understanding, not a lucky guess.

An interviewer asks: "Find all clients who have hired us for more than 3 projects." Which clause does the "> 3" condition belong in?

12. Summary & critical thinking

Two moves carried this whole reading: collapsing many rows into one summary row per group with GROUP BY, and filtering which of those summary rows survive with HAVING, a clause that only makes sense once an aggregate value already exists to test.

New situation: a community lending library logs every checkout: member_id, book_title, checkout_date. A branch manager wants to know which members have checked out more than 5 books this year. Which combination actually answers that?

13. Thinking ahead

A query that groups and filters like this one is often just the first half of a bigger question. Naming a query like this one, and letting a second query build on top of its result instead of repeating the whole GROUP BY and HAVING pattern inline, is the natural next step once both clauses feel automatic. The same skill of deciding "does this condition belong before or after aggregation" also comes back the moment ranking or per-group comparisons enter the picture.
