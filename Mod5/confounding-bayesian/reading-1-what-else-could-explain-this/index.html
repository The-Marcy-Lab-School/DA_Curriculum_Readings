What Else Could Explain This?

Eastbrook's free after-school tutoring program, Fifth Hour, looks like a success: students who attend graduate on time more often. Before the city triples its budget, an analyst asks two questions. What else is different about the students who attend? And as each month's numbers come in, what share of new students stop coming?

Skills you'll practice in this reading:

Bayesian Reasoning — Updating a belief as new evidence arrives: saying what you believed beforehand, what the data showed, and what you believe now, with the uncertainty still attached.

1. A 14-point gap

Fifth Hour meets in school libraries from 5:00pm to 6:30pm on weekdays. This spring, Eastbrook Public Schools pulled the numbers:

  1. 1,240 students attended Fifth Hour at least twice a week. 88% graduated on time.
  2. 2,060 students never attended. 74% graduated on time.

The 14-point gap is counted correctly. The question is what else differs between those two groups of students.

A council vote can turn on a gap like this, and nothing in the data flags the problem. You already know how to compare groups and read a p-value. This reading adds two separate habits: asking what sits behind a gap (part one), and updating a belief as data arrives in batches (part two).

3. Part one: what else is different about these students?

Think about who can be in the 1,240. A student who attends several days a week can be in a library at 5:00pm and get home after 6:30pm, all year. That usually takes an adult with a steady work schedule, a ride or a safe way home after dark, and a home address that stays the same all year.

Those same things also help a student finish school on time, with or without tutoring. So one thing pushes on both sides of the comparison: it shapes who attends, and it shapes who graduates.

A confounding variable is a hidden variable that drives both sides of a comparison, so a link between them looks real or causal when it might not be. Here, how steady a student's day-to-day life is acts as the confounder. Some unknown share of the 14-point gap belongs to it, not to the tutoring.

This is not about families doing anything wrong. A parent who works nights is not a worse parent. The problem is the comparison: the two groups differ in more than one way at once, so the data can't say which difference produced the gap.

What makes household stability a confounder here, and not just another column in the data?

What the confounder does and doesn't mean

Finding it does not prove Fifth Hour does nothing. It means this comparison can't answer the question. It also points to what would work better:

  1. A controlled comparison: compare students in similar circumstances, so the hidden variable is held about the same on both sides.
  2. A waitlist comparison: compare students who signed up and got a seat with students who signed up and didn't. Every family in both groups could sign up.
  3. Random assignment: give out seats by lottery. Chance then spreads every hidden variable evenly across both groups, so it can't explain a gap between them. This is the strongest option.

Sometimes a hidden variable does more than shrink a gap. In Simpson's paradox, the gap flips direction once you split the data by the hidden variable.

Having named the confounder, what should the analyst tell the city council?

5. Part two: updating a belief, one month at a time

Now a different question. Each month, a new group of students joins Fifth Hour, and some stop coming within their first month. The district wants to know what share of new students stop coming, and how sure it can be. Each month's group is one batch of data. (The numbers here are made up for practice.)

Before any data, the district's belief is wide open: the share could be close to 0 or close to 1. That starting belief is a prior: your belief about a true share before seeing the data at hand. After you fold in a batch, the updated belief is the posterior. The posterior from one month becomes the prior for the next.

A beta distribution is a standard way to write down a belief about a share, using two counters called alpha and beta. Starting both at 1 means every share from 0 to 1 is equally believable. The update rule:

  1. Add the number who stopped coming to alpha.
  2. Add the number who kept coming to beta.
  3. The best guess is alpha / (alpha + beta).

September: 40 joined, 6 stopped. October: 60 joined, 12 stopped.

New in this file: stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], alpha, beta) returns two numbers: the low and high ends of the middle 95% of the current belief. That range is a credible interval.

Predict: what number will dropout_belief.py print for alpha on the October line?

from scipy import stats alpha, beta = 1, 1 # the wide-open starting belief low, high = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], alpha, beta) print("start", round(alpha / (alpha + beta), 2), round(low, 2), round(high, 2)) months = [("September", 6, 40), ("October", 12, 60)] for month, stopped, joined in months: alpha += stopped beta += joined - stopped best = alpha / (alpha + beta) low, high = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], alpha, beta) print(month, alpha, beta, round(best, 2), round(low, 2), round(high, 2))
start 0.5 0.02 0.98
September 7 35 0.17 0.07 0.29
October 19 83 0.19 0.12 0.27

Each line prints the month, alpha, beta, the best guess, and the two ends of the 95% credible interval.

Two things happened. The best guess moved, from 0.50 to 0.17, then up to 0.19. October's rate, 12 out of 60, is 0.20, so the belief moved toward it without jumping all the way, because September is still in the counters. And the range got narrower: its width went from 0.95 to 0.22 to 0.15. More data shrinks the uncertainty.

After October, the best guess is 0.19 with a credible interval of 0.12 to 0.27. Which sentence is the honest one to bring the district?

6. Credible interval or confidence interval?

A 95% credible interval says there is a 95% chance the true share is inside this range. It can say that because it starts from a belief and updates it.

A confidence interval makes a claim about the method instead: if you repeated the study many times, 95% of the ranges built this way would contain the true value. It is not a 95% chance about the one range in front of you.

The district director asks: "What is the chance the true share is between 0.12 and 0.27?" Which kind of interval answers that question as asked?

9. Wrap-up

  • A confounder pushes on both sides of a comparison. Naming one means "this comparison can't answer the question," and it points to a better comparison, like a lottery.
  • A belief update starts from a prior, adds each batch to two counters, and gives a posterior that becomes the next prior.
  • Report the best guess with its credible interval. The interval narrows as data arrives, and only a credible interval supports "a 95% chance it's in here."

New situation: in November, 50 new students joined and 10 stopped coming. The belief is now a best guess of 0.19 with a 95% credible interval from 0.13 to 0.26. A council member wants one number for a slide. What is the best advice?

In interviews: "How do you tell correlation from causation?" is a common question, and a strong answer names a specific confounder and the comparison that would rule it out.

Where this goes next: once you can name a confounder, the next step is designing a comparison that closes it, with enough people to catch the effect you care about.
