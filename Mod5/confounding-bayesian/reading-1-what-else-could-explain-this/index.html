What Else Could Explain This?

Two reasoning habits, and they are not two versions of the same idea. The first one asks what else is different between two groups before you trust a gap between them. The second one asks how much a belief should move once real data starts arriving. You will work one real example of each: a city tutoring program with a graduation gap, and a city water lab narrowing in on a number, one batch of results at a time.

Skills you'll practice in this reading:

Experimental Design — Setting up a comparison so its result means something: choosing a sample size that can actually detect the effect you care about, and spotting a hidden variable driving a group difference.

Bayesian Reasoning — Updating a belief as new evidence arrives: saying what you believed beforehand, what the data showed, and what you believe now, with the uncertainty still attached.

1. Introduction

Eastbrook Public Schools is the public school system for a mid-sized city. For three years it has run a free after-school tutoring program called Fifth Hour, which meets in school libraries from 5:00pm to 6:30pm on weekdays. This spring the district pulled the numbers:

  • 1,240 students attended Fifth Hour at least twice a week. 88% graduated on time.
  • 2,060 students never attended. 74% graduated on time.

That is a 14 percentage point gap, from real records, correctly counted. The arithmetic is not in question. The city council wants to triple the program's budget on the strength of it.

There is one more move to make first, and it has nothing to do with the arithmetic. Ask what else is different between those two groups of students.

Why this matters: a council vote, a hiring plan, and somebody's grant renewal can all turn on one chart like that. Nothing in a dataset flags the problem for you. The gap is real, the percentages are right, and the spreadsheet looks finished. The only thing standing between that chart and a bad decision is an analyst who stops and asks what else the two groups differ on. "How do you tell correlation from causation?" is one of the most common questions in a data analyst interview, and the strong answer is never the slogan. The strong answer names a specific hidden variable and says what comparison would separate it out.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to split a table into groups and compare their averages, and you already know that a p-value tells you whether a gap is bigger than random variation would produce. Both of those skills are about the gap you can see. This reading is about two things neither one covers: what might be sitting behind the gap, and how a belief should move as evidence arrives in pieces instead of all at once. The second habit, in particular, opens the door to a very different way of reporting uncertainty than you have used so far.

Two habits, kept separate on purpose. Part one is about a hidden variable inside a comparison you already have. Part two is about updating a belief as new data arrives. They solve different problems, they use different tools, and they can be done independently of each other. Keep them in two different places in your notes.

3. Part one: what else is different about these students?

Go back to the Fifth Hour numbers and think about who can actually be in that 1,240.

A student who attends tutoring four days a week is a student who can be in a school library at 5:00pm and get home after 6:30pm, every single week, for a school year. That usually means some combination of a few things: an adult whose work schedule is predictable enough to plan around, a ride or a safe route home after dark, and an address that did not change in the middle of the year.

Now ask the second question. Do those same three things also help a student finish school on time, completely separately from any tutoring? Yes. A predictable household schedule, reliable transportation, and stable housing all make it easier to attend class, keep materials, and stay enrolled at one school.

So one thing is feeding both sides of the comparison at once. It shapes who ends up in the attended group, and it shapes graduation on its own. That thing has a name.

A confounding variable is a third thing that influences both of the things you are comparing, so the link you see between them is partly or entirely caused by it. In this case, the stability of a student's day-to-day circumstances is the confounder. It pushes students into the Fifth Hour group, and it pushes graduation up, so some unknown share of that 14-point gap belongs to it rather than to the tutoring.

Say this part carefully, because it is easy to get wrong. None of this is about families doing anything wrong, and none of it says anything about any student's ability. A parent working an overnight shift is not a worse parent. A family that moved twice this year is not a lesser family. The problem is not with the people in either group. The problem is with the comparison: the two groups differ in more than one way at the same time, so the data cannot tell you which difference produced the gap. That is a flaw in how the question was set up, not a flaw in anybody's household.

Here is the honest conclusion, and it is narrower than people usually expect.

  1. Finding this confounder does not prove the tutoring program does nothing. Fifth Hour might work very well. It might work a little. Nothing here rules either out.
  2. It does mean this particular comparison cannot answer the question. The attended and did-not-attend groups are not alike except for tutoring, so the gap between them mixes two effects together with no way to pull them apart.
  3. It also names what you would need instead. Three options, in rough order of strength:
    • A controlled comparison: compare students to other students in similar circumstances, so the hidden variable is held roughly constant on both sides.
    • A waitlist comparison: compare students who signed up and got a seat against students who signed up and did not, since everyone in both groups had a household able to sign up.
    • Random assignment: offer seats by lottery, so the two groups end up alike on everything, known and unknown, except the offer itself. This is the strongest option and often the hardest to arrange in a real public program.

Notice what option three buys you. A lottery does not make the hidden variable disappear. It just spreads it evenly across both groups, so it can no longer explain a gap between them.

What makes a student's household stability a confounder here, rather than just another column in the data?

7. Part two: a different habit, and a different question

Everything above was about one comparison you already had in hand. This part is about a question that shows up before you have much data at all: how much should a single batch of results move what you believe?

The city's water-quality lab tests drinking water for lead. Fern Hollow is a neighborhood with older service lines, the pipes that run from the street main into a building, so the lab is sampling taps there to find out what share of them come back above the city's action limit, the lead level at which the city is required to act. The lab tests in batches: a group of samples collected and run together over a few weeks.

The lab cannot test every tap in the neighborhood. It wants an honest answer to one question after each batch: what fraction of taps here are above the limit, and how sure are we?

The Fern Hollow numbers in this reading are made up for practice. Cities really do run tests like these, and the reasoning below is the real reasoning. The numbers are not a claim about any real neighborhood.

Before any data: the prior

Before the first batch comes back, what does the lab believe? Honestly, almost nothing. The true fraction could be close to zero, it could be high, and nothing collected so far rules anything out. A prior is exactly that: what you believe about an unknown number before you have seen the data at hand, including how sure you are.

To write a wide-open prior down, use a beta distribution, a standard way of representing a belief about an unknown fraction using two running counters, called alpha and beta. Start both at 1. That combination means every fraction from 0 to 1 is equally believable.

The update rule is short, and you apply it rather than derive it:

  1. Add the number of fails in the batch to alpha.
  2. Add the number of passes in the batch to beta.
  3. The best single guess is alpha / (alpha + beta).

What you get back after folding a batch in is the posterior: what you believe about that same number after the data. The posterior from one batch becomes the prior for the next one, which is what makes this work batch by batch instead of all at once.

Running it on the real batches

Two batches came back. Batch one: 40 taps sampled, 6 above the limit. Batch two: 60 taps sampled, 12 above the limit.

from scipy import stats

alpha, beta = 1, 1                     # the wide-open starting belief
low, high = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], alpha, beta)
print("before any data", round(alpha / (alpha + beta), 2), round(low, 2), round(high, 2))

batches = [("batch one", 6, 40), ("batch two", 12, 60)]

for label, fails, tested in batches:
    alpha += fails
    beta += tested - fails
    best = alpha / (alpha + beta)
    low, high = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], alpha, beta)
    print(label, round(best, 2), round(low, 2), round(high, 2))

batch one 0.17 0.07 0.29
batch two 0.19 0.12 0.27

stats.beta.ppf is the piece worth naming. It takes two cut points, here 0.025 and 0.975, and returns the two values that bracket the middle 95% of the current belief. That range is the credible interval.

Trace the counters once by hand and the whole mechanism stops being mysterious. alpha starts at 1, picks up 6 fails from batch one to reach 7, then picks up 12 more from batch two to reach 19. beta starts at 1, picks up 34 passes to reach 35, then 48 more to reach 83. Nothing else is happening.

Two different things happened in that picture, and they are worth separating.

  1. The best guess moved. It slid from 0.50 down to 0.17, then up slightly to 0.19. The first batch did most of the moving, because it arrived when the belief had nothing else holding it in place.
  2. The range tightened. Its width went from 0.95 to 0.22 to 0.15. That is the part people skip. More data did not just change the answer, it earned down the uncertainty around it.

The small upward move from 0.17 to 0.19 is worth a second look too. Batch two came back at 12 out of 60, a rate of 0.20, which is higher than batch one's 6 out of 40. The belief moved toward it without jumping to it, because batch one is still in those counters. That is the whole point of updating rather than replacing.

Before any samples came back, the lab's belief had a best guess of 0.50 and a range of 0.02 to 0.98. What is that wide range saying?

After batch two, alpha is 19. Where did 19 come from?

After batch two the belief is 0.19 with a credible interval of 0.12 to 0.27. Which sentence is the honest one to bring the water department?

8. Credible interval or confidence interval?

These two get used interchangeably all the time, including by working analysts. They are not the same claim, and the difference matters the moment a stakeholder asks a probability question.

The one sentence to be able to repeat back: a 95% credible interval says there is a 95% chance the true value sits inside this particular range, while a 95% confidence interval says that if you repeated the whole study many times, 95% of the ranges built that way would contain the true value, which is a statement about the method rather than about the one range in front of you.

The reason a credible interval can make the stronger claim is that it starts from a belief and updates it. The unknown fraction is treated as something you can hold a degree of belief about, so the finished belief can be sliced into a 95% range directly. A confidence interval never holds a belief about the unknown value at all, so it has nothing to make a probability statement about.

Both are useful. Only one answers "what is the chance the true number is in here."

The water department director asks: "What is the chance the true failure rate for Fern Hollow is between 0.12 and 0.27?" Which kind of interval actually answers that question as asked?

15. How this shows up in interviews

"What is the difference between correlation and causation?" is asked in data analyst interviews constantly, from first internship through senior roles. Almost everyone can recite the slogan, so the slogan earns nothing. What separates candidates is whether they can name a specific hidden variable in the specific example on the table, then say what comparison would settle it.

A strong answer has four beats. State what you observe in the data. Name the competing explanations, including one specific confounder. Say what evidence would separate them. Finish with a recommendation, which is usually the comparison or the experiment you would run next. Keep the whole thing concrete, because an interviewer is listening for whether you have actually had to do this.

Interview-style question: a hiring manager says, "Subscribers who opted into our weekly email renew at a much higher rate than subscribers who didn't. Should we auto-enroll everyone?" What is the strongest first answer?

16. Summary and critical thinking

Two habits, kept apart on purpose. The first one looks at a comparison you already have and asks what third thing might be pushing on both sides. Finding that thing does not prove the comparison is meaningless or that the program does nothing. It says this comparison cannot answer the question, and it names the comparison that could. The second habit looks at data arriving in pieces and asks how far a belief should move. You write down a prior, fold in a batch to get a posterior, report a credible interval around it, and let the next batch start from where you ended up.

Both habits share one underlying move: refusing to let a single number stand in for everything you know and do not know.

New situation: a community clinic is tracking what share of its text-message appointment reminders get a reply. After the first week's batch, its best guess is 0.52 with a 95% credible interval from 0.30 to 0.74. The clinic director wants one number for a board slide. What is the best advice?

17. Thinking ahead

Both halves of this reading leave a door open, and the two doors lead to different places.

On the confounding side, naming a hidden variable is the easy half. The hard half is designing a comparison that actually closes it, and then figuring out how many people you need in each group before that comparison could detect the effect you care about. A study too small to find a real effect produces the same empty result as a study where nothing is there, which is a trap worth knowing about before you design anything.

On the belief side, the two batches here were only a starting point. Once the posterior from one batch becomes the prior for the next, you have a procedure that runs forever: every new week of results sharpens the number without ever throwing the earlier weeks away. The same machinery handles a click-through rate, a defect rate, or a reply rate. Getting comfortable with the idea that a belief carries its own uncertainty, and that uncertainty shrinks only as fast as the data earns it, makes a lot of later statistics feel like one idea instead of twelve.
