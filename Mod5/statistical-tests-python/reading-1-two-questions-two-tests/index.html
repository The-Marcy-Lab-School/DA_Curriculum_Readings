Two Questions, Two Tests

A small bakery chain hung a new menu board in half its shops for one month and kept the old board in the other half. The owner comes back with two questions about the same pile of receipts: did people spend more, and did people order different things? Those are two different questions about two different kinds of data, and they need two different statistical tests.

1. Introduction

Rise & Shine Bakehouse is a small chain of six neighborhood bakeries. A menu board is the big sign hanging above the counter that lists everything for sale. For one month, three of the shops kept the old board, which we'll call Layout A. The other three hung a new board, Layout B, which groups items by meal instead of by price.

At the end of the month the owner has 985 receipts and two questions:

  1. Did people spend more per order under the new board?
  2. Did people order different things under the new board?

Those look like one question asked twice. They are not. "How much did someone spend" is a number. "What did someone order" is a label: pastry, sandwich, or drink only. A number and a label are two different kinds of data, and the kind of data you have decides which test you are allowed to run.

Why this matters: picking a test is not a formality you get graded on in a stats class. It is the step that decides whether the owner reprints six menu boards or leaves them alone. Run the wrong test and Python still hands you a p-value that looks completely normal. Nothing turns red. Nobody gets an error message. You just quietly report a conclusion the data never supported, and the chain spends real money on it. This is also the single most common statistics question in a data analyst interview: "how do you decide between a t-test and a chi-square test?"

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know what a null hypothesis claims, what a p-value measures, and what a confidence interval describes. This reading is about turning that vocabulary into three lines of real Python, on real data, and then reading what comes back without overselling it. Once running the test feels routine, the harder question waiting on the other side is how big a real difference has to be before anyone should act on it.

3. Concept overview: one month of receipts, two kinds of data

Every receipt in the bakery's file carries the same handful of facts: which layout that shop had, what the order total was in dollars, and what kind of order it was. Here are the first few rows:

orders (first 4 of 985 rows)
layoutorder_totalorder_type
A6.25pastry
A12.80sandwich
B3.50drink-only
B11.40sandwich

Look at the two columns on the right. order_total holds dollar amounts. An amount can sit anywhere on a scale: $3.50, $6.25, $12.80, or anything between. A column like that holds a continuous variable, a measurement on a scale where the values are real numbers you can average.

order_type is different. Its values are names: pastry, sandwich, drink-only. You cannot average them. There is no such thing as the mean of "pastry." A column like that holds a categorical variable, a variable whose values are labels or groups rather than numbers on a scale.

That one distinction decides everything that follows.

  1. To compare an average number between two groups, you run a t-test. Here: is the average order_total different for Layout A than for Layout B?
  2. To check whether two categories are related to each other, you run a chi-square test. Here: is order_type related to layout, or do the two layouts produce the same mix of orders?

Both tests live in the same place: SciPy, a free Python library of scientific and statistical tools, and specifically its scipy.stats section. Both also need the data loaded first, which is pandas, the Python library for working with tables of data. Loading the file looks the same either way:

import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

orders = pd.read_csv("bakehouse_orders.csv")
print(orders.shape)
(985, 3)

orders.shape reports 985 rows and 3 columns, which matches the 985 receipts the owner handed over. That is worth checking every single time, before any test runs. A test cannot tell you that half your data failed to load.

5. Question 1: did the average order total change?

Start by writing down the null hypothesis, in plain words, before any code runs: the average order total is the same under both layouts, and the gap in this month's receipts is just ordinary random variation. Writing it first matters. It is very easy to talk yourself into a story after you have already seen the answer.

Now pull the two groups apart and hand them to SciPy.

layout_a = orders[orders["layout"] == "A"]["order_total"]
layout_b = orders[orders["layout"] == "B"]["order_total"]

print("Layout A mean:", round(layout_a.mean(), 2))
print("Layout B mean:", round(layout_b.mean(), 2))

result = stats.ttest_ind(layout_a, layout_b)
print("t-statistic:", round(result.statistic, 2))
print("p-value:", round(result.pvalue, 2))
print("95% CI:", result.confidence_interval(confidence_level=0.95))
Layout A mean: 9.42
Layout B mean: 10.01
t-statistic: -2.19
p-value: 0.03
95% CI: (-1.12, -0.06)

ttest_ind is the two-sample t-test: "ind" is short for independent, meaning the two groups are made of different people, not the same people measured twice. It subtracts the second group's average from the first, so everything it reports is about Layout A minus Layout B. Layout A's average is lower, which is why the numbers come back negative.

The p-value is 0.03. If the two boards really produced the same average order, a gap this big would show up about 3 times in 100 just by chance. That is small enough that most teams would stop calling it noise.

The confidence interval is where the honest reading happens. .confidence_interval() is a real method on the object ttest_ind hands back, and it returns the 95% interval for that same difference.

Read that band carefully, because it says two things at once. Every value inside it is negative, so the data is not consistent with Layout B being worse or with the two boards being identical. But the band runs from $1.12 all the way down to $0.06. The real gain per order might be a dollar and change, or it might be six cents. The p-value alone would never have told you that.

Given a 95% confidence interval of -$1.12 to -$0.06, which sentence is the honest one to bring the owner?

The p-value came back as 0.03. What does that number actually measure?

6. Question 2: did the mix of orders change?

Second question, same receipts, completely different shape of data. Nobody is averaging anything now. The question is whether order_type and layout are related at all.

The null hypothesis, written first again: the two layouts produce the same mix of pastry, sandwich, and drink-only orders, and any difference in this month's counts is ordinary random variation.

A chi-square test cannot read a long list of labels. It needs a contingency table first: a small table that counts how many orders fall into each combination of the two categories. One category runs down the rows, the other runs across the columns, and every cell holds a plain count. In pandas, pd.crosstab builds one in a single line.

table = pd.crosstab(orders["layout"], orders["order_type"])
print(table)
order_type  drink-only  pastry  sandwich
layout
A                  115     210       155
B                   97     199       209

That is the whole month in six numbers. Layout A's 480 orders split 24% drink-only, 44% pastry, 32% sandwich. Layout B's 505 orders split 19% drink-only, 39% pastry, 41% sandwich. Fewer people walked out with just a drink, and more walked out with a sandwich. That is a plausible explanation for the higher average order total from the first test, since a sandwich costs more than a coffee.

The eye can see a pattern there. The test decides whether the pattern is bigger than random variation would produce.

chi2, p, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(table)

print("chi-square statistic:", round(chi2, 2))
print("p-value:", round(p, 2))
print("degrees of freedom:", dof)
print("expected counts:")
print(expected.round(2))
chi-square statistic: 9.21
p-value: 0.01
degrees of freedom: 2
expected counts:
[[103.31 199.31 177.38]
 [108.69 209.69 186.62]]

chi2_contingency hands back four things at once, so the four names on the left of the equals sign get filled in left to right. Take them one at a time.

  1. The chi-square statistic (9.21) measures total distance between what you counted and what you would have expected if the two categories were unrelated. Bigger means further from "unrelated."
  2. The p-value (0.01) says a gap this large would show up about 1 time in 100 if the layouts really produced the same mix. Random variation is a weak explanation here.
  3. Degrees of freedom (2) is the count of cells that were still free to change once the row and column totals were locked in. The table has 6 cells, but fill in 2 of them and every remaining cell is forced by the totals. The test needs that number to judge whether 9.21 is large.
  4. Expected counts is the table the test compared against: the counts you would see if layout had nothing to do with order type. Layout A "should" have had about 177 sandwiches and actually had 155. Layout B "should" have had about 187 and had 209.
observed counts next to expected counts
layoutdrink-onlypastrysandwich
A observed115210155
A expected103.31199.31177.38
B observed97199209
B expected108.69209.69186.62

Notice what the test does not say. It says layout and order type are related. It never says the board caused the change. Something else could have moved with the boards: the three shops that got Layout B might sit closer to offices, or the new board might have gone up the same week a sandwich supplier changed. Association is what a chi-square test measures, and association is all it measures.

The table has 6 cells but only 2 degrees of freedom. Why?

The chi-square p-value is 0.01. In one plain sentence, what has the owner actually learned?

11. Running a test in an order you can defend

The honest caveat, and it is a big one: both of these tests come with assumptions about the data, and neither one checks them for you. A t-test assumes the two groups are roughly bell-shaped with no wild outliers. A chi-square test assumes every expected count in the table is at least about 5, which is exactly why chi2_contingency hands back that expected table. Break either assumption and nothing visible goes wrong. SciPy still returns a number that looks like a perfectly ordinary p-value, formatted the same way, sitting in the same variable. It just does not mean what you think it means. The only protection is checking first, every time, because the output cannot warn you.

12. Watch: choosing the right version of a t-test

The bakery's two groups are made of different customers, which is why ttest_ind was the right call. As you watch, notice the one question that decides everything: were these the same people measured twice (which calls for a paired t-test), or two separate sets of people (which is what ttest_ind is for)? The video also recommends a version of the t-test that does not assume both groups have the same spread. That is one extra argument in SciPy, stats.ttest_ind(layout_a, layout_b, equal_var=False), and it is a safe default when you are not sure.

14. How this shows up in interviews

"How do you decide between a t-test and a chi-square test?" is asked constantly in data analyst interviews, from first internship through senior roles. Interviewers like it because the answer separates someone who memorized two test names from someone who has actually had to pick one.

A strong answer leads with the rule, not the test names: "it depends on the data type of what I am comparing." Then one concrete example of each. A t-test when the outcome is a number I can average, like order total in dollars across two groups. A chi-square test when both variables are labels, like order type against which menu board a shop had. Finish by naming the check you would run before trusting either result, and you have said everything the question was really asking.

Interview-style question: a hiring manager says, "We surveyed 400 riders. We want to know whether the share of riders choosing the monthly pass instead of pay-per-ride is different for riders under 30 versus 30 and over. Which test, and why?"

15. Summary and critical thinking

Two ideas carried this whole reading. First, the kind of data decides the test: a number you can average points at a t-test, two sets of labels point at a chi-square test built on a contingency table. Second, a test result is three numbers, not one. The p-value says whether random variation is a believable explanation. The confidence interval says how large the real difference plausibly is. Your own sentence says what any of it means for the decision in front of someone.

New situation: an online bookstore ran two versions of its checkout page for three weeks. The team wants to know whether the share of visitors who finished checkout (finished or abandoned) differed between the two versions. Which test fits, and what is the null hypothesis?

16. Thinking ahead

Both results here cleared the usual bar, and both left the same question wide open. The t-test said the gap in average order total is probably not zero. It never said the gap is worth acting on. Six cents an order and a dollar an order are both "probably not zero," and only one of them pays for reprinting six menu boards. The chi-square test has the same gap in it: the mix of orders is related to the board, but "related" covers everything from a barely visible shift to a total reversal.

That missing piece has its own name and its own numbers, separate from the p-value entirely. Getting comfortable with the idea now, that "is it real" and "is it big" are two different questions with two different answers, makes the rest of statistical testing much easier to hold together.
