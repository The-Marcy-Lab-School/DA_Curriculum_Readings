Could This Just Be Chance?

A job-training nonprofit ran two versions of its resume workshop. The graduates of version B got more callbacks from employers. The gap looks like good news. Before anyone rewrites the program, there is one question to settle: did version B actually work better, or did that gap show up by chance in these particular groups of people?

1. Introduction

Bridgeway Skills Collective is a job-training nonprofit. A nonprofit is an organization paid for by donations and grants instead of by selling something, and this one teaches free classes in job skills to adults who are looking for work. One of its most popular classes is a one-day resume workshop.

Last spring the staff tried something. Half the incoming students got the usual workshop, which they called version A. The other half got version B, which added a practice round at the end where a volunteer from a hiring team reads your resume out loud and says what stood out.

Six months later the staff counted callbacks. A callback is when an employer gets back in touch after an application, usually to set up an interview. Here is what they found.

  1. Version A: 120 graduates, 42 of them got at least one callback. That is 35.0%.
  2. Version B: 118 graduates, 52 of them got at least one callback. That is 44.1%.

Version B is ahead by 9.1 percentage points, which is just the plain subtraction of one percentage from the other: 44.1 minus 35.0. Everyone in the room wants to celebrate. And it might really be worth celebrating. But a second explanation is sitting right next to the first one, and it has to be ruled out before anyone acts.

Why this matters: a real organization is about to spend real money on this answer. Version B costs more: longer sessions, volunteers to recruit and train, fewer students per class. If the 9.1-point gap is real, that cost buys people interviews. If the gap is just the luck of who signed up in which month, the nonprofit spends its budget on nothing and serves fewer people while doing it. Getting callbacks depends on plenty of things nobody in that room controls, like who is hiring that month and how many people applied to the same posting. The analyst's whole job here is to separate the part the program can claim from the part it cannot.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to describe one group of data, and how to put two groups' averages or percentages side by side and see which is higher. This reading adds the step that comes after that comparison: deciding whether the difference you just spotted is strong enough to act on. Once that logic is solid, the code that computes these numbers for you is a short step, and it will make sense instead of just printing digits.

3. Two explanations for the same gap

Picture one big jar filled with marbles, some blue and some white, all mixed together. Now scoop that jar into two smaller jars at random. Count the blue marbles in each.

They will not match. One jar will always have a slightly higher share of blue than the other, and if you scoop again you will get a different pair of numbers. That difference is completely real, in the sense that you can go count it. It also says nothing whatsoever about the marbles, because both jars came out of the same place.

The 9.1-point gap at Bridgeway has exactly two possible explanations, and the marble jars are the second one.

  1. Version B really is better. The practice round genuinely helps people get callbacks, and the gap in the data reflects that.
  2. Both groups came out of the same jar. The two versions work equally well, and 238 people happened to split in a way that put a few more eventual callbacks on the B side. Scoop again next spring and it could tilt the other way.

A hypothesis test exists to weigh explanation 2 before anyone commits to explanation 1. It starts by taking explanation 2 completely seriously, and giving it a name.

Null hypothesis: there is no real difference between the two versions, and any gap in this data is ordinary random variation, the marble-jar kind.

Alternative hypothesis: there is a real difference between the two versions.

Two things about that pair surprise most people the first time.

First, you write both of them down before you look at any result. Written afterwards, a hypothesis is just a description of what you already saw, and it can be quietly reshaped to match. Written first, it is a real claim that the data can push back on.

Second, the test only ever puts pressure on the null hypothesis. It can find evidence strong enough to reject the null, or it can fail to find that evidence. What it can never do is prove the null true. "We did not find enough evidence to rule out chance" is an honest sentence. "We proved the two versions are the same" is not, and the gap between those two sentences is where a lot of bad decisions get made.

5. What the p-value actually said

The Bridgeway staff ran the comparison. The p-value came back as 0.15.

Here is what that number means, stated the long way, because the short ways people use are usually wrong:

If the two versions really worked equally well, a gap at least as big as 9.1 percentage points would turn up about 15 times in 100, just from the luck of who ended up in which group.

Read that sentence twice. Every part of it is doing work.

  1. "If the two versions really worked equally well" is an assumption the calculation starts from, not something it concludes. A p-value always begins by pretending the null hypothesis is true.
  2. "a gap at least as big as" means the calculation counts this gap and every bigger one, not just this exact result.
  3. "about 15 times in 100" is how often that would happen across many reshuffles of the same kind of people.

So 0.15 is a statement about how ordinary or unusual this data looks in a world where the null is true. That is the only thing it is.

The two wrong versions, said out loud on purpose, because they sound almost identical to the right one and you will hear both of them at work.

"There is a 15% chance the null hypothesis is true." No. The calculation assumed the null was true in order to produce 0.15 in the first place. It cannot then turn around and tell you the odds on that same assumption.

"There is a 15% chance this result was a fluke." Also no, and this one is sneakier because it sounds humble. It still describes a probability about your conclusion, when 0.15 describes how often data like this appears under an assumption.

Most teams use 0.05 as the line where they stop calling something chance. At 0.15, Bridgeway is well above that line. The honest sentence is: this data does not give strong evidence that version B works better. Notice what that sentence does not say. It does not say version B is useless. It does not say the two versions are the same. It says the evidence here is not strong enough to rule chance out.

8. The confidence interval: the range the data is consistent with

A p-value answers one narrow question: is chance a believable explanation? It says nothing at all about how big the difference might be. For that you need the other number every test can give you.

A confidence interval is the range of values the data is consistent with. For Bridgeway, the 95% confidence interval for the difference in callback rate runs from -3.3 percentage points to +21.4 percentage points, with 9.1 as the single best estimate in the middle.

Read the two ends. The top end says version B might be worth a full 21 points, which would be a genuinely large win for the people in that class. The bottom end is negative, which means the data is also consistent with version B being slightly worse. And somewhere in between sits zero, which means "no difference at all" is still on the table.

That is the whole story of a result that is not convincing. It is not that the answer is no. It is that this data is consistent with nearly everything, from a small loss to a large gain, and an answer that wide cannot guide a decision.

A rule worth memorizing: if the confidence interval for a difference includes zero, the result is not convincing, no matter how encouraging the headline number looks. A 95% confidence interval that includes zero and a p-value above 0.05 are two views of the same fact.

9. Two ways a conclusion can be wrong

Whatever Bridgeway decides, it could be wrong, and there are exactly two ways to be wrong. They have plain names and plainer consequences.

  1. Type I error, a false alarm. You call the difference real when nothing was actually there. Bridgeway rolls version B out everywhere, pays for the volunteers and the longer sessions, and callback rates never move.
  2. Type II error, a miss. A real difference was there and your study did not catch it. Bridgeway shelves version B, and a change that genuinely would have helped people get interviews quietly disappears.

Neither error can be removed. Pushing hard against one tends to raise the other: demand very strong evidence before believing anything and you will miss more real effects, while accepting weak evidence gets you more false alarms. Choosing 0.05 as your cutoff is a statement about how much false-alarm risk you are willing to carry.

It is tempting to treat a miss as the safer mistake, since at least nothing false got claimed. It is not safer for the person who would have gotten the interview.

10. How big does a sample have to be?

Here is the number that reframes Bridgeway's whole result. With about 120 people in each group, that study was only big enough to reliably catch a difference of roughly 17 percentage points or more. That figure has a name: the minimum detectable effect, the smallest difference a study is actually sized to catch.

The gap they were hoping to find was 9.1 points. The study was never built to detect a gap that size. It was, in a real sense, set up to come back inconclusive no matter what was true.

To reliably catch a 9-point difference, the same comparison would need roughly 460 graduates in each group, not 120. That is a very different study: a different timeline, a different budget, possibly a partnership with other sites.

Three things push the required sample size around.

A smaller difference you are trying to catch needs a bigger sample. A larger, more obvious difference needs a smaller one. And the more people naturally vary on the outcome you are measuring, the more of them you need, even at the same difference size.

All of which means "how many people do we need?" has no answer on its own. It only becomes answerable once someone says how small a difference would still be worth acting on.

A different nonprofit plans to test a small change it expects to raise job placements by about 3 percentage points, using 60 people in each group. What is the real risk?

  • Name the smallest difference that would actually change what the program does.
  • Write the null and alternative hypotheses down, before any data exists.
  • Work out how many people each group needs to catch a difference that size.
  • Run the comparison on the people you actually enrolled.
  • Report the confidence interval alongside the p-value, never the p-value alone.

11. When a huge sample makes a tiny gap "significant"

Now run the problem the other direction. Bridgeway belongs to a national network of job-training nonprofits. The network pooled six years of results from hundreds of sites and ended up with 60,000 graduates on each version of the workshop. Same two versions, same callback question, vastly more people.

Look at that table for a second, because it is genuinely strange the first time. The study with the bigger gap came back not significant. The study with the tiny gap came back significant. Nothing went wrong in either one.

Sample size is the reason. A huge sample makes an estimate very precise, and a very precise estimate can rule out zero even when the real difference is minuscule. Go back to the diagram above and look at the second band. The network study is confident: the real difference is somewhere between 0.06 and 1.14 percentage points, and it is not zero. It is also confident that the difference is almost nothing.

Put that 0.6-point gap in human terms. Out of every 1,000 graduates, version B produces about 6 more people with a callback. That might be worth it, if version B is cheap. It is almost certainly not worth it if version B means recruiting a volunteer for every single class, everywhere, forever. That question is not a statistics question at all, and no p-value has an opinion on it.

This is the difference between statistical significance, meaning chance is a poor explanation, and practical significance, meaning the difference is big enough to be worth acting on. A new bus route that gets you to work 8 seconds sooner is real, measurable, and repeatable. Nobody moves apartments over 8 seconds. Both things can be true at once, and a large enough study will hand you exactly that combination on a regular basis.

The network study reports a significant 0.6-point gain. A director wants to make version B mandatory at every site. What is the most useful question to ask first?

Why did the smaller study's larger 9.1-point gap come back not significant, while the huge study's 0.6-point gap did?

One thing a bigger sample will never fix. More people shrinks random, group-to-group variation. It does nothing at all to a problem baked into how the groups were formed. If version B was offered on evenings and weekends and version A during the workday, the two groups differ in who could attend, and every extra person you add carries that same tilt. A bigger sample then reports the wrong answer more precisely, which is worse than reporting it uncertainly.

15. How this shows up in interviews

"Explain a p-value to someone who is not technical" is one of the most common data analyst interview questions there is, from first internship through senior roles. Interviewers keep asking it because the wrong answer is so easy to give fluently.

A strong answer stays in plain words and keeps the assumption in front: "it is how surprising my result would be if there were really no difference at all." Then a concrete example, and then the part that actually earns the point: naming what it is not. "It is not the chance my conclusion is wrong, and it is not the chance the result was a fluke." Finish by saying you would report the confidence interval next to it, because the p-value alone never tells anyone how big the difference is.

Interview-style question: a hiring manager says, "Our last test came back with a p-value of 0.60, so we concluded the two versions perform the same and moved on." How do you respond?

16. Summary and critical thinking

Three ideas carried this reading. First, any gap between two groups has two explanations, and the null hypothesis is the name for the boring one you have to rule out before claiming the interesting one. Second, a p-value only ever answers "how ordinary would this data look if there were no difference at all," while the confidence interval answers the separate and often more useful question of how big the difference plausibly is. Third, a sample can be too small to catch anything real, and it can also be so large that it certifies a difference far too small to act on. Both failures produce a perfectly normal-looking number on screen.

New situation: a nonprofit that runs free coding classes wants to know whether a new mentor-matching system raises the share of students who finish the course. 17. Thinking ahead

Everything here treated the comparison itself as settled: two groups, one outcome, one number to compare. Real questions rarely arrive that tidy. Sometimes the outcome is a dollar amount you can average, sometimes it is a yes or no you can only count, and sometimes there are three groups instead of two. The logic of the null hypothesis, the p-value, and the confidence interval stays exactly the same across all of those. What changes is the specific calculation underneath, and picking the right one is its own skill.

The habit worth carrying forward is smaller and more useful than any single test: name the smallest difference that would change your mind before you look at the data, and report the interval next to the p-value every time. Do those two things and most of the mistakes in this reading stop being available to you.
