# Research Archive: Liberatory Pedagogy for Data Education

Raw research findings behind `pedagogy/liberatory_pedagogy.md` — kept as a full
reference archive (not read by any script) so the primary sources and full
reasoning are available if this guidance needs revisiting or extending later.
Gathered via a WebSearch-enabled research pass, 2026-07-17. General culturally
relevant/sustaining pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, Paris) and stereotype-threat
mitigation research are deliberately out of scope here — that ground is already
covered in `pedagogy/engagement_techniques.md` and `pedagogy/relatable_analogies.md`.

## Thread 1 — Foundational liberatory pedagogy theory

**1. Freire, P. (1970/2000). *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*. Continuum (trans. M.B.
Ramos). (High confidence — foundational, canonical text.)**

Freire's "banking model" describes education as depositing static information
into passive students who are treated as empty receptacles — a structure that
mirrors and reinforces top-down social hierarchy. His alternative,
"problem-posing education," starts from learners' lived experience and uses
dialogue to jointly investigate contradictions and injustices in that
experience, with the teacher functioning as a co-investigator rather than a
depositor of facts. *Conscientização* (critical consciousness) is the process
by which people come to perceive the world as a constructed, changeable reality
rather than a fixed given. *Praxis* is Freire's insistence that reflection
divorced from action is merely verbalism, and action divorced from reflection
is mere activism — real transformation requires the cycle of both together.

**Translation:** A "liberatory-feeling" slide/lesson poses a real, open problem
drawn from students' own context rather than only stating a finished fact;
check-for-understanding and discussion prompts should function as joint
investigation, not just retrieval; every unit should close with a praxis step —
a concrete action, not just a reflection.

**2. hooks, b. (1994). *Teaching to Transgress: Education as the Practice of
Freedom*. Routledge. (High confidence.)**

hooks extends Freire with "engaged pedagogy" — teaching that attends to the
whole student (mind, body, spirit), not just intellect, and treats the
classroom as a site that can either reproduce or disrupt domination. Her
concrete recommendations: the instructor must be self-actualized and visibly
invested in their own growth, not a detached authority; learning should never
be merely dutiful but should hold real excitement; multiculturalism and
students' full identities must be actively welcomed rather than tolerated; and
pedagogy should aim at "the practice of freedom," not just credentialing.

**Translation:** Motion notes / delivery guidance in a slide deck should model
instructor vulnerability and enthusiasm, not detached delivery; lesson framing
should explicitly welcome students' full selves (career-changer identity,
cultural background) as assets, not distractions.

## Thread 2 — Liberatory pedagogy applied to teaching mathematics/data specifically

**3. Gutstein, E. (2003). Teaching and learning mathematics for social justice
in an urban, Latino school. *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education*,
34(1), 37–73. See also Gutstein, E. (2006). *Reading and Writing the World with
Mathematics: Toward a Pedagogy for Social Justice*. Routledge. (High
confidence.)**

Gutstein directly operationalizes Freire in a math classroom: students "read
the world" (understand issues of justice and power) and "write the world" (act
on that understanding) using mathematics as the analytic tool — e.g., using
real data/statistics to investigate rent, policing, or unemployment patterns in
students' own neighborhoods. His two-year study found this raised both
students' "mathematical power" and their orientation toward the subject.

**Translation:** Data-analytics case studies and exercises should, where
possible, use datasets that let learners investigate conditions in communities
like their own (housing, policing, health, wages) rather than only
generic/corporate sample data — this is a direct, literal template for this
Fellowship's "real public datasets" convention already in
`pedagogy/industry_domains.md`.

**4. Gutiérrez, R. (2018). Introduction: The need to rehumanize mathematics. In
I. Goffney, R. Gutiérrez, & M. Boston (Eds.), *Rehumanizing Mathematics for
Black, Indigenous, and Latinx Students* (NCTM Annual Perspectives in
Mathematics Education). National Council of Teachers of Mathematics. (High
confidence.)**

"Rehumanizing" argues math teaching has historically dehumanized marginalized
students and proposes eight dimensions for redesign, including "Windows and
Mirrors" (students should see both their own identity reflected back and views
into other worlds), "Bodies and Emotions," "Broadening Mathematics" (whose
knowledge counts as mathematical), and "Ownership and Stewardship." It has
direct curriculum-design implications, not just a stance.

**Translation:** Concept-map/case-study slides should function as both mirror
(visibly reflecting learners' backgrounds/communities in examples) and window
(exposing them to other contexts); "whose knowledge counts" implies validating
non-institutional data/ways-of-knowing learners already bring in, not only
textbook methods.

**5. Milner, Y., & Traub, A. (2021). *Data Capitalism and Algorithmic
Racism*. Data for Black Lives & Demos. https://www.demos.org/research/data-capitalism-and-algorithmic-racism
(Confidence note: this is a published policy report, not peer-reviewed academic
literature — citable as case-study source material, not as an academic
pedagogical framework.)**

Documents real cases of algorithmic racism (facial recognition misidentification,
discriminatory ad targeting, etc.) and argues data science should be reoriented
toward racial-justice accountability.

**Translation:** Usable as real-world case-study source material illustrating
the stakes of biased data/algorithms — pairs with `case_study` slides on data
ethics/bias — but it does not itself supply lesson-design principles; use Data
Feminism (below) for the pedagogical framework and this as concrete
real-world-harm examples to cite.

**6. D'Ignazio, C., & Klein, L. F. (2020). *Data Feminism*. MIT Press. (High
confidence — well-known, widely cited text.)**

Seven principles usable for curriculum: examine power, challenge power, elevate
emotion and embodiment, rethink binaries and hierarchies, embrace pluralism,
consider context, and make labor visible. Concretely relevant to a
data-analytics curriculum: "consider context" argues data is never
neutral/raw and should always be taught with its collection conditions and
limitations disclosed; "make labor visible" argues the human labor (including
undervalued/invisible labor, often by marginalized workers) behind a dataset or
pipeline should be named, not erased; "elevate emotion and embodiment" pushes
back against a "view from nowhere" objectivity, treating students' emotional/
lived reactions to data as legitimate analytic input.

**Translation:** Every data-analytics case study or dataset slide should
include: who collected this data and why, what/whose experience is missing
from it, and whose labor produced it — a literal, checkable slide-design item.

## Thread 3 — Empirical backing for Black/Latino young adults in coding/data bootcamps

**7. Byrd, A. (2025). *Black Tech Ecosystems: How Black Adult Learners Use
Computer Code Bootcamps for Liberation*. The WAC Clearinghouse & University
Press of Colorado. DOI: 10.37514/WRI-B.2025.2630. (High confidence — this is
the closest direct match found to this Fellowship's actual population and
context.)**

A year-long ethnographic study of low-income Black adult learners (18–58 years
old, mixed genders) at a real nonprofit coding bootcamp explicitly framed
around social mobility and anti-racism. Findings are mixed and instructive
rather than triumphant: participants developed rich "Black coding Discourse"
and communal carework practices that sustained them through training, but
post-graduation, **most did not land software-development jobs**; racial
microaggressions, stereotype threat, and whiteness-coded professionalism norms
(in curriculum and hiring) undercut the liberatory promise even when
instructors' intent was genuinely equity-minded. Liberation showed up less as
job placement and more as reclaimed self-definition, mutual carework, and
redirected literacy toward community-serving ends.

**Translation — this is a required caution, not just a finding:** "liberatory"
framing in a slide/lesson should not promise upward mobility as the payoff, but
should validate carework, community, and self-definition as legitimate wins
even when the labor market doesn't cooperate; instructor-modeled empathy and
explicit acknowledgment of workplace bias (rather than pretending a "neutral"
meritocracy) is itself a documented liberatory practice with adult learners in
exactly this population.

### Confidence assessment — Thread 3

**Thin but real.** There is exactly one strong, recent (2025), directly
on-point ethnographic monograph (Byrd) studying Black adult learners in a
coding bootcamp — a near-exact analog to this Fellowship's population and
setting. Beyond that single study, empirical backing specific to Black/Latino
*young adults* (not K-12 students) in *technical/data* workforce-development
contexts is genuinely sparse; most of what exists (adjacent dissertations/
studies on gender or mixed-race bootcamps) addresses different populations
rather than this one precisely. Everything in Threads 1, 2, and 4 is
extrapolated — reasonably, given shared mechanisms (concreteness, real-stakes
relevance, dialogic trust) — from K-12 math-education research and general
critical-pedagogy theory, not verified with this specific age/context group.
Treat the Byrd study as a legitimate, citable anchor for this population, but
do not present the broader liberatory-pedagogy literature as if it has been
empirically validated for adult coding/data bootcamps generally — it hasn't.

## Thread 4 — Concrete slide/lesson design translation

Drawing directly from the sources above, not restated theory:
1. Phrase key conceptual slides as an open problem or question rooted in a real
   stake, rather than only a declarative definition (Freire/Gutstein's
   "problem-posing" pattern).
2. Tie at least one example or dataset per unit to conditions in students' own
   or comparable communities (Gutstein's read/write-the-world method).
3. For any dataset or case study, disclose collection context, missing
   perspectives, and whose labor produced it (Data Feminism's "consider
   context"/"make labor visible").
4. End units or modules with a concrete action/application step, not just a
   reflection prompt (Freire's praxis requirement).
5. Notes/motion-note guidance to instructors should model visible personal
   investment and should openly name industry bias/stakes rather than
   presenting a sanitized meritocracy (hooks's engaged pedagogy and Byrd's
   bootcamp findings).
6. Frame diagrams/examples so students both see themselves reflected and see
   beyond their existing context (Gutiérrez's "windows and mirrors").
