The Loudest Number in the Room

A small reading program for kids held a fundraising dinner. Eleven guests gave gifts. Ten gave between $40 and $75. One company gave $5,000. The program's leader asks one question: "What is a typical gift, so we can set next year's goal?" The honest answer depends on which number you pick.

1. Eleven gifts, one question

3. Three ways to say "typical"

A number that describes the middle of a list is called a measure of center. There are three common ones.

  • The mean is the sum of all values divided by how many values there are. Most people call it "the average." recall_total.py worked out a mean.
  • The median is the middle value once the list is sorted. With 11 sorted gifts, the middle one is the 6th, which sits at index 5.
  • The mode is the value that shows up most often. Here, $50 appears three times, so the mode is $50. A list can have one mode, several, or none.

New in this file: sum(gifts) adds every number in the list, the same job as the loop in recall_total.py. round(mean, 2) keeps 2 digits after the decimal point.

Predict: gift_center.py prints two lines. What does the second line, the median, show?

gifts = [40, 45, 50, 50, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 5000] mean = sum(gifts) / len(gifts) median = gifts[5] print("mean:", round(mean, 2)) print("median:", median)
mean: 505.45
median: 55

The mean is about $505. Not one of the eleven guests gave close to $505. Ten gave less than $80, and one gave $5,000. The median, $55, sits right among the ten smaller gifts, where most guests gave.

4. One far-off value pulls the mean

An outlier is a value that sits far away from the rest of the data. The $5,000 gift is an outlier. Here is a test: what happens if you take it out of the list?

The ten smaller gifts add up to $560.

  1. Divide $560 by the number of gifts left, which is 10.
  2. Type only the number, like 48. Leave out the $ sign.
  3. Press Check my prediction. You get 2 tries. Then the Run button unlocks so you can compare.

With 10 values, there is no single middle value, so the median is the mean of the two middle values: the 5th and 6th, at index 4 and index 5.

gifts = [40, 45, 50, 50, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75] mean = sum(gifts) / len(gifts) median = (gifts[4] + gifts[5]) / 2 print("mean:", mean) print("median:", median)
mean: 56.0
median: 52.5

The rule: the mean uses the size of every value, so one outlier can drag it a long way. The median only cares about which value sits in the middle, so an outlier barely moves it.

5. Same average, different spread

The center is only half the story. You also want to know how spread out the values are. The standard deviation is a typical distance between each value and the mean. A small standard deviation means the values sit close to the mean. A large one means they are spread far apart.

At the dinner, guests sat at tables. Table A gave $55, $58, $60, $62, and $65. Table B gave $20, $40, $60, $80, and $100. Both tables have a mean of $60.

6. The shape of the data

Sort a list and cut it into four equal parts. The three cut points are called quartiles. Q1 is the median of the lower half. Q3 is the median of the upper half. The median itself sits between them.

Those three cut points plus the smallest and largest values make the five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum. For the eleven gifts, it is $40, $50, $55, $70, $5,000. (Tools like pandas use a slightly different method for quartiles, so their Q1 and Q3 can differ by a few dollars.)

The distance from Q1 to Q3 is the interquartile range, or IQR. It covers the middle half of the data.

  1. Subtract Q1 ($50) from Q3 ($70).
  2. Type only the number, like 15. Leave out the $ sign.
  3. Press Check. You get 2 tries.

The middle half of the gifts spans only $20, yet the maximum sits $4,930 above Q3. That gap is a sign of skew. Skew means a distribution leans to one side, with a long tail of values stretching the other way. The gifts have a long tail to the right, so they are right-skewed. In a right-skewed list, the tail pulls the mean above the median, just like $505 and $55. A left-skewed list has its long tail on the left. A symmetric list looks about the same on both sides.

A histogram shows shape as bars: each bar counts how many values fall in a range. Find the tall bars, then find the long, low tail.

8. Wrap-up

  • Mean, median, and mode each describe "typical" in a different way. The mean gets pulled by outliers; the median barely moves.
  • Standard deviation tells you how spread out values are. Two lists can share a mean and still be very different.
  • The five-number summary and a histogram show shape. A long tail on one side is skew.
