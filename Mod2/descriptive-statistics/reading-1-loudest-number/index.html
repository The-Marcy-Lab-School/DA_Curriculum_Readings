The Loudest Number in the Room

A youth orchestra held a dinner to raise money. Eleven gifts came in. Ten guests gave between $40 and $75. A local company gave $5,000. The organizer asks, "What is a typical gift, so we can set next year's goal?" One number says $505. Another says $55. Only one of them describes the people in the room.

2. What is typical? Mean and median

Every number in this reading answers one of two questions: what is typical, and how spread out are the values? Start with typical.

New in this file: import numpy as np loads NumPy and lets you call it np. np.array([...]) turns a list into an array, NumPy's version of a list that does math on every value at once. np.mean and np.median work out the mean and the median. .round(2) rounds a NumPy number to 2 decimal places.

Predict: what does gift_center.py print?

import numpy as np

gifts = np.array([50, 40, 65, 50, 75, 45, 60, 50, 70, 55, 5000])

print(np.mean(gifts).round(2))
print(np.median(gifts))
505.45
55.0

The mean adds every gift and divides by 11. The $5,000 gift pulls it up to $505.45. The median is the middle gift once they are sorted: $55. It ignores how far away the $5,000 is. A third measure, the mode, is the value that shows up most often. Three guests gave $50, so the mode is $50.

A quick test: only one of the 11 gifts is at or above the mean of $505.45. Neither number is wrong math. The question is which one describes a typical guest.

Also good to know: Python's built-in statistics module has mean() and median() for a plain list, with the same answers.

The organizer wants to print "Our typical gift is ___" on next year's invitation. Which number fits best?

3. How spread out? Standard deviation and the five-number summary

Someone planning around a number also needs to know how far the values wander from it.

New in this file: np.std(gifts, ddof=1) works out the standard deviation. ddof=1 picks the "sample" formula, the same one pandas uses. np.percentile(gifts, [0, 25, 50, 75, 100]) gives the five-number summary in one call. NumPy prints the five values as an array, and 40. means 40.0.

Predict: after you run gift_spread.py, which of the five numbers in its second line flags something unusual?

import numpy as np

gifts = np.array([50, 40, 65, 50, 75, 45, 60, 50, 70, 55, 5000])

print(np.std(gifts, ddof=1).round(2))
print(np.percentile(gifts, [0, 25, 50, 75, 100]))
1490.71
[  40.    50.    55.    67.5 5000. ]

The five-number summary is the minimum, Q1, median, Q3 and maximum. Each quartile marks a split: a quarter of the gifts are at or below Q1 ($50), and three quarters are at or below Q3 ($67.50). From Q1 to Q3 is only $17.50. From Q3 to the maximum is almost $4,933. A gap that big marks an outlier, a value far from the rest.

The standard deviation is the typical distance between a gift and the mean. Here it is $1,490.71, larger than almost every gift. A big standard deviation can mean real variety, or one far-off value. The five-number summary shows which one you have.

If you find the quartiles by hand, you might get Q3 = $70, the middle of the upper half. NumPy estimates between values and gets $67.50. Neither is wrong. When numbers will be compared, say which method you used.

4. Shape: which way do the values lean?

A histogram is a bar chart where each bar counts how many values fall in a range.

Skew is the way the values lean. In a symmetric shape, the mean and median sit close together, so the mean is a fair summary. A right-skewed shape has a long tail of big values, which pulls the mean above the median. A left-skewed shape has a tail of small values, which pulls the mean below the median. Money is often right-skewed, so look for a tail before you choose the mean.

The gifts are right-skewed. Should you delete the $5,000 gift? Try it as a test with the ten guests only.

New in this file: gifts[:-1] is a slice: every item except the last one. The $5,000 gift is last, so people_only holds the ten guests' gifts.

  1. Sort the ten guests' gifts in your head: 40, 45, 50, 50, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75.
  2. Find the median. With ten values, it is the average of the 5th and 6th values.
  3. Type only the number, such as 48.5, with no dollar sign. Then press Check.

Why: one median by hand shows why a far-off value can't move it much.

import numpy as np

gifts = np.array([50, 40, 65, 50, 75, 45, 60, 50, 70, 55, 5000])
people_only = gifts[:-1]

print(np.mean(people_only).round(2))
print(np.median(people_only))
print(np.std(people_only, ddof=1).round(2))
56.0
52.5
11.25

Without the company, the mean drops by about $450. The median moves by less than $5. The standard deviation shrinks from $1,490.71 to $11.25, so one value made it more than 100 times bigger.

But the $5,000 is real money, so it stays in the data. Report the median as the typical gift, and mention the company's gift on its own line.

6. Wrap-up

  • The mean gets pulled toward far-off values. The median barely moves. On skewed data, the median describes a typical value.
  • Report spread next to a center. The standard deviation and the five-number summary show how far values wander.
  • Look at the shape, then pick the number. Keep a real outlier, and mention it on its own.
