One Row Per Group

A regional blood donation nonprofit runs donation drives at library branches, fire stations, and community centers across its service area, logging one row per drive: the site, how many donors registered, how many units were actually collected, and how many volunteer hours it took to run. After a few months the program coordinator has dozens of rows and one real question: which sites are actually worth running again? Scrolling through every row by hand doesn't scale, and neither does retyping the same formula into a new column over and over. This reading is about summarizing many rows into one number per group, and building a new column instead of recalculating one by hand.

1. Concept overview

Picture the coordinator's spreadsheet. Every donation drive is its own row: which site hosted it, how many donors showed up, how many units of blood were actually collected, and how many volunteer hours it took to staff. Six drives in, the raw table looks like this:

site donors_registered units_collected volunteer_hours Riverside Library 34 30 6 Eastside Community Center 22 20 5 Downtown Fire Station 55 50 10 Riverside Library 44 40 8 Eastside Community Center 28 25 5 Eastside Community Center 33 30 6

Three sites, six rows, mixed together in whatever order the drives actually happened. Answering "how many units did each site collect in total" by scrolling and adding by hand works for six rows. It falls apart at six hundred. groupby() is pandas' answer: it gathers every row that shares the same value in one column into its own group, without changing the original DataFrame at all.

That's the whole mechanism, in three steps pandas actually calls split, apply, combine: split the rows into groups sharing a value, apply an aggregation function separately inside each group, then combine the per-group results into one row per group. In code, once you've decided which column to aggregate:

drives.groupby("site")["units_collected"].sum() [Output] site Downtown Fire Station 50 Eastside Community Center 75 Riverside Library 70 Name: units_collected, dtype: int64

Swap .sum() for .mean() or .count() and the split step doesn't change at all, only what happens inside each group during "apply."

The second half of this reading is a different move that fits right alongside it: building a brand-new column out of columns you already have, instead of recalculating the same formula by hand every time you need it. If each drive's efficiency matters (units collected per volunteer hour, say), that's one line: drives["units_per_volunteer_hour"] = drives["units_collected"] / drives["volunteer_hours"]. Pandas runs that division on every row at once and stores the result as a real column, right there in the DataFrame.

Why this matters: "which segment is actually driving this number" is one of the most common asks in a real data role, whether it's a program director asking which site to visit again or a manager asking which region drove this quarter's result. Grouping is the mechanical answer, and it's a standard first move in almost every real exploratory analysis, not a specialized trick reserved for advanced work.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to read a DataFrame and pull out the rows you want with a boolean filter, like drives[drives["units_collected"] > 30]. This reading is about summarizing many rows at once instead of looking at them one at a time, and building a new column from data you already have instead of retyping the same formula. Once grouping and deriving columns both feel automatic, the natural next step is using them together to dig for a pattern that isn't obvious just from glancing at the raw numbers.

6. Multiple summaries at once with .agg()

Calling .sum(), then .mean(), then .count() separately means splitting the same six rows into groups three separate times. .agg() does the split once and applies every function you list:

drives.groupby("site")["units_collected"].agg(["count", "mean", "sum"]) [Output] count mean sum site Downtown Fire Station 1 50.0 50 Eastside Community Center 3 25.0 75 Riverside Library 2 35.0 70

10. Building a derived column

A total tells you volume. It doesn't tell you efficiency. To compare how much each drive collected relative to the volunteer time it took, the coordinator needs a column that doesn't exist yet: units collected per volunteer hour. Building it is one line, run once across every row at the same time:

drives["units_per_volunteer_hour"] = drives["units_collected"] / drives["volunteer_hours"]

Pandas performs that division row by row and stores the result as a real column, right there in drives. No loop, and nothing typed in by hand.

A common gotcha

If drives is itself a filtered slice of a bigger DataFrame, for example this_year = full_log[full_log["year"] == 2026], assigning a new column onto it can trigger pandas' SettingWithCopyWarning. The warning doesn't always stop your code from running, but it means pandas isn't sure whether you're editing the real data or a temporary view. The fix is calling .copy() right when the filtered slice is created: this_year = full_log[full_log["year"] == 2026].copy().

11. Total vs. efficiency: digging past the obvious number

Running units_per_volunteer_hour on all six rows gives:

site units_collected volunteer_hours units_per_volunteer_hour Riverside Library 30 6 5.0 Eastside Community Center 20 5 4.0 Downtown Fire Station 50 10 5.0 Riverside Library 40 8 5.0 Eastside Community Center 25 5 5.0 Eastside Community Center 30 6 5.0

Eastside Community Center collected the most units overall (75, from three drives) and would look like the strongest site by total alone. But averaging its own units_per_volunteer_hour column tells a different story: (4.0 + 5.0 + 5.0) ÷ 3 ≈ 4.67, pulled down by one weaker drive, while Riverside Library and Downtown Fire Station both average a clean 5.0.

Which site actually had the LOWEST average collection efficiency per volunteer hour, even though it collected the most total units and ran the most drives?

"How would you find total sales per region from a table of individual orders?" is one of the most common warm-up prompts in a data-role technical screen, whether it's asked as a pandas question or its SQL cousin (a GROUP BY with SUM()). 15. Summary & critical thinking

Two moves carried this whole reading: collapsing many rows into one summary row per group with groupby, and building a new column out of columns you already have instead of recalculating the same formula by hand. Together, they're how a question like "which site is actually most efficient" gets answered without eyeballing anything.

16. Thinking ahead

This same pair of moves scales up fast. Grouping by more than one column at once, like site and month together, checks whether a pattern holds across time as well as location, and deriving a column from a groupby result itself, comparing each row to its own group's average, is a natural next step once both of these feel automatic. They're also the actual mechanism behind mining a dataset for a pattern that isn't obvious from the raw numbers: the exact move used to go from a pile of individual rows to a genuinely useful finding.
