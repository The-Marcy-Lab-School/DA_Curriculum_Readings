The Pattern Isn't the Proof

A food bank delivers grocery boxes to people's homes in four neighborhoods. At the Lakeview site, 41% of deliveries are no-shows: nobody was home. Every other site is under 20%. The math is right, and the food bank wants to cut Lakeview's deliveries. First, ask one more thing: how was this number recorded?

1. A right answer from shaky data

Each site logged more than 100 deliveries last quarter, so the 41% is not a fluke from a few rows. But correct math only proves the counting was right. It says nothing about how each driver decided what counts as a no-show.

If the food bank cuts deliveries because of a number nobody checked, real families lose groceries. You already know how to find a pattern and check that enough rows back it up. In this reading, you check the step that came before the data: how it was collected.

How the examples work: each pandas example is a .py file that reads a small .csv file. The Run button shows what the file prints. On your own laptop, you'd type python3 name.py in the terminal.

3. Ask where the number came from

The collection process is the real-world steps, people, and tools that made a dataset: who typed each value, using what form, and under what rules. Two sites can use the same word, "no-show," and mean different things by it.

A worry like "is this data accurate?" can't be answered. Nobody can look up one fact that settles it. A checkable question is specific enough that the person who owns the data could answer it with a concrete fact, such as a rule, a form, or a date.

Before Lakeview's 41% cuts anyone's deliveries, which question is checkable?

A teammate's notes say only: "The data seems mostly reliable." What is the biggest problem with that sentence?

4. Is every group covered the same way?

Completeness means a dataset covers what it claims to cover. A few blanks spread evenly across sites are normal. Blanks piled up in one group are worth asking about, because that group's numbers were recorded differently.

When a driver logs a no-show, the form has a reason box. no_shows.csv holds 12 no-shows from the log. An empty spot after the comma means the driver left the reason blank, and pandas reads it as NaN.

Predict: what count does blank_reasons.py print for Lakeview?

site,reason Lakeview, Lakeview, Lakeview,not home Lakeview, Lakeview, Riverside,at work Riverside,not home Eastgate,wrong address Eastgate, Midtown,not home Midtown,at work Midtown,wrong address
import pandas as pd no_shows = pd.read_csv("no_shows.csv") blank = no_shows[no_shows["reason"].isna()] print(blank["site"].value_counts())
site
Lakeview    4
Eastgate    1
Name: count, dtype: int64

Four of Lakeview's five no-shows have no reason, while Riverside and Midtown have none blank. That uneven gap is a lead. Maybe Lakeview drivers skip the form when they're in a hurry. If so, a checkable question is: "Do Lakeview drivers get the same training on the no-show form as drivers at other sites?"

5. Who is missing, and what else changed?

Three more problems come from how data was gathered, not from the math:

  • Selection bias: the data has too many or too few people from some group because of how it was gathered, not because of anything true about that group.
  • Survivorship bias: a kind of selection bias where you only see the cases that made it through an earlier step. Whatever made the others drop out stays hidden.
  • Confounding variable: a hidden third factor that could explain a pattern instead of the reason people are giving for it.

Eastgate's average satisfaction score is 3.1 out of 5. The other sites score 4.2 to 4.5. Eastgate hands its survey only to people who pick up boxes in person. People who get boxes delivered are never asked. Which problem is this?

7. Wrap-up

  • Correct math on badly collected data still gives a misleading number.
  • Check completeness group by group. Blanks piled up in one group are a lead.
  • Turn each worry into a checkable question that someone could answer with a fact.

New situation: the food bank's Midtown site has a new manager. Midtown's no-show rate fell from 18% to 9% the next month. Someone wants to give the new manager the credit. What's the strongest next move?

In interviews: "What would you ask about a dataset before trusting a finding from it?" is a common early question, and a checkable question is a strong answer.

Where this goes next: once you trust a number, the next job is explaining it, and its limits, to someone who never saw the data.
