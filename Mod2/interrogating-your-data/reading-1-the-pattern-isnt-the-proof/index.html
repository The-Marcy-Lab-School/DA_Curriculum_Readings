The Pattern Isn't the Proof

You already found a real pattern in a dataset: a clean, correctly computed difference between two groups. Second Chance Pantry Network's Lakeview site shows a no-show rate more than double every other site's, and a program director is ready to act on it. Nothing about the arithmetic being right tells you whether the number itself deserves that much trust. This reading is about the move that comes next: interrogating where a number came from before it drives a real decision.

1. Concept overview

Picture Second Chance Pantry Network, a nonprofit that delivers grocery boxes to homebound and low-mobility clients across four neighborhoods: Riverside, Eastgate, Midtown, and Lakeview. Digging through last quarter's scheduling data, you found a real pattern: Lakeview's no-show rate, the share of scheduled deliveries where nobody was home to receive the box, sits at 41%. Riverside, Eastgate, and Midtown all land between 14% and 19%. Every site logged well over 100 scheduled deliveries last quarter, so this isn't a tiny-sample fluke, and the arithmetic behind the 41% is completely correct.

The program director wants to act on it fast: cut Lakeview's delivery slots in half and reroute the driver hours to the other three sites. Before a number like that drives a decision that affects real people's groceries, there's one more move, and it has nothing to do with the arithmetic. It's asking where the number actually came from.

Why this matters: a data-quality memo that raises zero real questions about where its numbers came from is one of the fastest ways a data project reads as unfinished to a reviewer. Outside of school, the stakes are higher: a real decision like cutting delivery slots, funding, or staffing, made on a pattern nobody double-checked at the source, can cost real people real service, not just a grade.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to check whether a pattern survives a closer look inside the data itself: comparing it against a hidden third factor, confirming the sample behind it is large enough to mean something. This reading is about a different kind of check, one that happens before either of those: not whether the arithmetic holds up, but whether the process that produced the numbers deserves your trust at all. Once asking that question is automatic, it applies to any dataset you're ever handed, not just one you built yourself.

2. Recall before interrogating

Two skills from earlier work are assumed going forward: recognizing what actually counts as a real pattern, and knowing that a pattern's reliability depends partly on how much data backs it up. Test both below before this reading moves to a different kind of check entirely.

In a dataset like Second Chance's scheduling log, a "pattern" worth taking seriously is best described as:

Scenario check: Site A: 3 no-shows out of 7 scheduled deliveries (43%). Site B: 38 no-shows out of 210 scheduled deliveries (18%). Which comparison deserves more skepticism, purely because of how much data backs it up?

Critical thinking: true or false. If the arithmetic behind a percentage is completely correct, the finding it produces can't be misleading.

Neither of these goes away once you start interrogating a data source, they still matter. But this reading is about a third check, separate from both: even a large-enough sample with solid arithmetic can still come from a collection process that makes the number untrustworthy.

  • Confirm the pattern is a real, correctly computed difference across groups.
  • Ask exactly how the relevant numbers were recorded, and by whom.
  • Check whether every group was covered as completely as every other group.
  • Make sure the question is specific enough that a real data owner could answer it with a fact.
  • Only then decide how much weight the pattern deserves in a real decision.

13. How this shows up in interviews

A take-home data project that presents a finding with zero acknowledgment of its data's limitations is one of the most visible red flags a reviewer sees, more visible than a rough chart or a typo. It reads as someone who found a number and stopped, not someone who thought about what the number could and couldn't actually support.

Expect a direct version of this in an interview: "Tell me about a time you questioned a dataset before trusting a finding from it" is a real, increasingly common question in data interviews. The specific dataset matters far less than showing a repeatable habit: naming exactly what you'd want to know about how the data was collected before you'd stake a recommendation on it.

A live version shows up too: an interviewer hands you a chart or a summary stat and asks, "what would you want to know about how this was collected before you presented it to a client?" The expected answer is a specific, checkable question, the same skill practiced in sections 5 and 6, not a general disclaimer like "more research is needed."

Interview-style: you're handed a chart showing a company's returning-customer rate jumped 20% after a new loyalty app launched. What's your first move?

14. Build your interrogation habit

A teammate's data-quality memo says only: "The data seems mostly reliable." What's the single biggest problem with that sentence?

15. Summary & critical thinking

Two habits carried this whole reading: treating a correctly computed pattern as unproven until its collection process has been checked, and turning a vague worry into a specific, checkable question a real person could answer.

New situation: a job-training nonprofit's satellite location shows a 38% six-month job-retention rate for graduates, next to 71% at its downtown location. What's the strongest next move?

16. Thinking ahead

Interrogating a collection process before trusting a pattern comes up anywhere a number is about to drive a real decision: reading a vendor's usage report before renewing a contract, checking a survey's response rate before citing it in a proposal, or asking how an app's crash reports are gathered before trusting what they say is the top bug.
