Beneath the Total

FixIt is a phone repair shop with two locations, Downtown and Eastside. The owner sees that Downtown repairs take about 15 minutes longer on average, and asks, "Is Downtown slow?" The average is correct. But it may not mean what it seems to mean.

Your goal: split a number by two columns at once, check what each group is made of, and find a pattern worth telling the owner. Acting on a wrong pattern can cost money, like retraining a team that was never slow. You already know how to group by one column and when a median beats a mean.

2. One average can hide what is inside it

Here are FixIt's 12 repairs from one week. Each row is one repair: the shop, the repair type, whether the phone is old or new, and how many minutes it took.

shop,repair,phone_age,minutes Downtown,screen,old,55 Downtown,screen,new,45 Downtown,battery,old,24 Downtown,screen,old,58 Downtown,screen,new,44 Downtown,screen,new,48 Eastside,battery,old,25 Eastside,screen,old,56 Eastside,battery,new,17 Eastside,battery,new,16 Eastside,screen,new,46 Eastside,battery,old,23
import pandas as pd
repairs = pd.read_csv("repairs.csv")
repairs.groupby("shop")["minutes"].mean().round(1)
shop
Downtown    45.7
Eastside    30.5
Name: minutes, dtype: float64

Downtown looks slow. Before you believe it, ask: what else is different about Downtown? Count the mix: which repair types each shop did.

Downtown did 5 screen repairs and 1 battery repair. Eastside did mostly battery repairs. Screen repairs take much longer, so the mix alone could explain the gap.

3. Compare the same job across shops

To test whether the shop matters, compare the same repair type at both shops.

Predict: for screen repairs only, how do the two shops compare?

repairs.groupby(["shop", "repair"])["minutes"].mean().round(1)
shop      repair
Downtown  battery    24.0
          screen     50.0
Eastside  battery    20.2
          screen     51.0
Name: minutes, dtype: float64

For screen repairs, Downtown takes 50.0 minutes and Eastside takes 51.0. The 15-minute gap was a confound: the shop was standing in for the repair type. Also notice that Downtown did only 1 battery repair. One repair is too few to trust, so don't compare that number.

4. Segment by two columns and find a real pattern

"Screen repairs take longer than battery repairs" is true, but the owner already knows it. A non-obvious pattern is real, checkable, and something the owner would act on. To look for one, segment the minutes by repair type and phone age together.

New in this cell: .agg([...]) can include "std", the standard deviation. The outer parentheses let one chain of steps run over several lines.

Predict: do old phones take longer than new phones in both repair types?

(
    repairs.groupby(["repair", "phone_age"])["minutes"]
    .agg(["count", "mean", "median", "std"])
    .round(1)
)
                   count  mean  median  std
repair  phone_age
battery new            2  16.5    16.5  0.7
        old            3  24.0    24.0  1.0
screen  new            4  45.8    45.5  1.7
        old            3  56.3    56.0  1.5

Old phones take about 8 minutes longer for a battery and about 10 minutes longer for a screen. The gap shows up in every repair type, so it is not a fluke in one group. The owner can act on it, for example by giving longer wait times for old phones. The counts are small, so the next step is to check that the gap holds over more weeks.

Which statistic should back up the old-phone finding? Look at the mean, median and std columns in cell [4].

6. Wrap-up

  • One average can hide groups that differ a lot. Split it before you trust it.
  • When one group looks much better or worse, count its mix with .size(), then compare the same kind of row across groups.
  • A finding worth reporting holds in every group, rests on enough rows, and uses the statistic that fits: the mean when it is close to the median and the std is small.

New situation: next month, Eastside's average repair time is 12 minutes higher than Downtown's. The owner wants to move a worker. What should you check first?

In interviews, "The overall average looks fine. Why split by group?" is a common question. A strong answer says an average can hide groups that differ, and gives an example like this one.

A pattern you found still needs questions about where the data came from and what it leaves out, before anyone acts on it.
