Beneath the Total

Riverbend Mutual, a small regional auto insurer, hands a new data analyst a quarter's worth of claims: which region filed each one, what type of claim it was, whether the policy carries a roadside-assistance add-on, how many days it took to close, and how much it paid out. The VP of Claims asks one question: "What's actually going on with our claims costs this quarter?" It would be easy to answer with the total. It's a real number, and it's true. It's also not a finding, just a fact anyone could have pulled from a spreadsheet without really looking at anything. This reading is about telling the difference between a number that restates what's already visible and a pattern that's actually worth someone's attention.

1. Concept overview

Picture the spreadsheet the VP just handed over: 240 rows, one per claim, with columns for region (metro or rural), claim type, whether the policy carries a roadside-assistance add-on, days to close, and payout amount. The easiest thing to do with 240 rows is add them up: $700,800 paid out this quarter, an average of $2,920 per claim. True. Reportable. And exactly what anyone would expect to hear, because it doesn't say anything about how or why. A single overall number describes the whole dataset at once, which means it can't say anything about how the dataset differs from itself. Restating a total, or restating an already-known fact ("collision is our most common claim type" is true, and also something everyone in the room already assumed), is what this reading means by surface-level.

Mining for a real pattern means doing something a total can't: splitting the data into segments and asking whether those segments actually differ from each other. Region is one plausible segment here, plausible because a regional insurer might reasonably expect its rural and metro service areas to behave differently. Grouping by region and comparing the averages is one line:

claims.groupby("region")["payout_amount"].mean() [Output] region metro 2300.0 rural 3850.0 Name: payout_amount, dtype: float64

Metro claims average $2,300 across 144 claims. Rural claims average $3,850 across 96 claims, about 67% higher, even though rural claims make up fewer than half of everything filed this quarter. That's a real comparison, backed by a real slice of the data on each side, and specific enough that the VP could actually act on it: something about rural claims costs more, and someone should find out what.

Three things separate that rural-vs-metro gap from the plain total sitting next to it. First, it's a comparison between at least two groups, not one number standing alone. Second, the gap is a size a reasonable person would call meaningful, not a rounding difference that could just as easily be noise. Third, and easiest to skip past: a stakeholder who already knows the business wouldn't have already assumed it. "Collision is our most common claim type" fails that third test even though it's a real, checkable fact about the data, because everyone in the room already knew it before the analysis started.

That third test is also where sample size sneaks back in. A gap only counts as a finding once it's built on a reliable slice of the data: enough rows that the number would probably look similar if pulled again, not two or three claims that happened to be expensive. A pattern built on a handful of rows can look exactly as dramatic as a real one and still not be trustworthy, which is worth checking before repeating a number to a stakeholder, not after.

Why this matters: almost every real data role eventually asks some version of "what did you actually find?" A report that only hands back a total or an already-known fact reads as busywork, not analysis, and stakeholders notice the difference fast. Learning to tell a real, comparison-backed finding apart from a restated number is one of the most transferable judgment calls in the job.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to group rows by a category and compute a mean, median, or count for each group, and how to filter a DataFrame down to the rows you care about. This reading doesn't add new syntax to any of that. It's entirely about the judgment call that comes after the syntax: which segment is even worth grouping by, how big a gap has to be before it means something, and how to tell a pattern a stakeholder would find genuinely useful apart from a fact they already knew.

4. Predict, then check: a second segment

Region isn't the only plausible segment sitting in the claims data. Riverbend Mutual also tracks whether a policy includes a roadside-assistance add-on, and how many days each claim took to close.

Predict first: based only on the fact that a policy includes roadside assistance, do you expect claims tied to it to close faster, slower, or about the same as claims without it?

claims.groupby("has_roadside_assistance")["days_to_close"].mean()

Payout amounts for the two groups turn out to be nearly identical, within about $40 of each other, which is exactly what makes the closing-time gap worth a second look instead of being explained away by cost alone.

10. Order the steps: the mining process

Click steps, in order, to build the process actually used above to go from a total to a real finding. Click a step again to remove it if you change your mind, then press "Check my order" when ready. This is a self-check, not graded on correctness.

  • Name the surface-level fact everyone would already restate: a total or an overall average.
  • Pick a segment that might plausibly explain a difference, not just any column that happens to exist.
  • Compute each segment's number and compare it against the other segment(s).
  • Check whether the gap is actually built on enough rows to trust, not a handful of records.
  • State the finding by naming the segment and the size of the gap, not just repeating the overall number.

12. How this shows up in interviews

"Walk me through an interesting finding you made in a dataset" is one of the single most common prompts in a data-analyst interview, and a vague or surface-level answer is an immediate red flag. Interviewers aren't just checking whether you touched real data, they're checking whether you can tell a restated total apart from something that actually required digging, and whether you checked the gap held up before repeating it.

A near-guaranteed follow-up: "how did you know it wasn't just a coincidence?" A confident answer names the sample size or the comparison you checked it against. "I just thought it looked interesting" is exactly the kind of answer this reading's reliable-slice check exists to prevent.

14. Summary & critical thinking

Two habits carried this whole reading: telling a genuine comparison-based finding apart from a total or an already-known fact restated as if it were news, and checking that a real-looking gap is actually built on enough rows to trust. Together, they're what turns "here's a number from the data" into "here's something worth changing because of the data."

15. Thinking ahead

Mining a dataset for a real pattern and then questioning whether that pattern deserves your trust are two different skills. Once spotting a genuine, comparison-backed finding starts to feel natural, the next real question turns toward the data underneath it: where it actually came from, how complete it is, and what would make someone reasonably skeptical of trusting it at all.
