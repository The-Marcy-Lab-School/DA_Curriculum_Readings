Drop, Fill, or Flag

Riverside Community Health Clinic's front-desk tablet logs every walk-in visit: wait time, blood pressure, insurance type, a callback number. By the end of a busy month, the export has real gaps in it: a few rows that came back completely blank from a sync glitch, some blood pressure readings a rushed nurse never took, insurance types missed on the clinic's busiest, most understaffed day, and callback numbers plenty of patients never gave in the first place. Every one of those gaps needs an answer, and "just drop the nulls" is not one answer, it's four different decisions wearing the same shrug.

1. Concept overview

Picture the export sitting in front of you: 4,000 rows from this month at Riverside, one row per visit, columns for wait time, blood pressure, insurance type, and a callback phone number. Four different gaps show up in it, and they are not the same problem wearing four faces.

About 12 rows came back entirely blank, every column empty, including the automatically generated timestamp. That's a known glitch when the tablet syncs mid-visit. There is nothing usable left in those rows at all, which is exactly when you reach for drop: removing a row (or column) outright with .dropna(), because whatever remains genuinely cannot be used or trusted.

Now picture 40 rows missing systolic_bp, because a nurse occasionally skips vitals during a rush. The rest of each row is real: a real visit, a real wait time, a real insurance type. For a monthly aggregate report, a typical blood pressure value is a defensible stand-in for the few it's missing. That's fill, also called imputation: replacing a gap with a computed value, usually a column's mean, median, or mode, via .fillna().

Now picture 300 rows missing insurance_type, concentrated on Saturdays when the clinic runs with a skeleton front desk. Guessing the most common insurance type here would not be a neutral stand-in, it would likely misrepresent exactly the patients the report is trying to describe. That's flag: instead of guessing, mark which rows were missing with a new boolean column (built from .isna()), so the gap stays visible rather than getting quietly papered over.

# Drop the rows that are entirely blank df = df.dropna(how="all") # Fill a numeric gap with the column's median median_bp = df["systolic_bp"].median() df["systolic_bp"] = df["systolic_bp"].fillna(median_bp) # Flag a gap instead of guessing, before it gets filled df["insurance_missing"] = df["insurance_type"].isna() df["insurance_type"] = df["insurance_type"].fillna("Unknown")

Why this matters: "how do you handle missing data" is one of the most common data-analyst interview questions there is, and it's graded on judgment, not on knowing that .dropna() and .fillna() exist. Outside the interview, a careless fill or drop quietly distorts a report before anyone notices, since a mean built from filled-in guesses looks exactly as confident on a chart as one built from real numbers.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to filter, group, and build derived columns on data that's already clean. This reading is about the judgment call that comes before any of that reliably works: deciding what a specific gap in a specific column should become. Once that decision gets made correctly, column after column, the next real problem is doing it without retyping the same fix by hand every time, which is exactly what a reusable cleaning function solves.

4. Decide: drop, fill, or flag

You're building this month's operations report for Riverside's clinic manager: an aggregate summary of wait times, vitals, and visit volume by insurance type, not any single patient's chart. Four real gaps turned up in the intake log. For each one, decide, and be ready to justify it.

Scenario 1: about 12 of the 4,000 logged visits are rows where every single field, including the automatically generated timestamp, came back blank. It's a known tablet glitch during syncing. What should you do with these 12 rows?

Scenario 2: systolic_bp is missing for 40 of the 4,000 visits, because a nurse occasionally skips vitals during a rush. The recorded values cluster tightly around a typical range with no major outliers.

Scenario 3: insurance_type is missing for about 300 of the 4,000 visits, concentrated heavily on Saturdays, when the clinic runs with a skeleton front desk. The report needs a breakdown of visit counts by insurance type.

Scenario 4: follow_up_phone has always been an optional field on the intake form, and it's missing for about 1,800 of the 4,000 visits.

11. How this shows up in interviews

"How do you handle missing data?" is one of the most reliable data-analyst interview questions there is, precisely because a vague answer is such a clean red flag. "I just drop the nulls" or "I fill everything with 0" both signal that missing data gets a reflex instead of a decision. A strong answer names the actual reasoning: how much is missing, whether the gap looks random or patterned, and what a wrong guess would cost in that specific column.

Interviewers often follow up with a scenario instead of stopping at the general question: "this column is missing 40% of its values, what do you do?" The strongest response usually starts with a question back, what does this column represent, and what happens downstream if the fill is wrong, rather than reaching for one blanket rule for every column in the dataset.

13. Summary & critical thinking

One question carried this whole reading: for this specific gap, in this specific column, is there nothing worth keeping (drop), a safe typical value (fill), or a gap that shouldn't be guessed at (flag)? Getting to the right answer means asking what the column represents and what's actually missing, not reaching for the same fix every time.

New situation: a small nonprofit's volunteer sign-up sheet has a phone_number column empty for 15% of entries (never required on the form), and a shift_hours column empty for 3% of entries where a volunteer forgot to log their hours after a real shift. Which column is the stronger candidate to fill with a typical value?

14. Thinking ahead

Once a drop, fill, or flag decision gets made correctly for one column, in one file, the more common problem is doing that same fix reliably across many columns, or the same messy file arriving again next month, without copy-pasting the same lines over and over. Turning a one-off fix into a real, reusable function that takes a DataFrame and a column name as arguments is the natural next skill once these decisions stop being one-time judgment calls and start being something worth writing once and trusting every time after.
