Drop, Fill, or Flag

Paws & Go is a dog-walking app. Each row in its log is one walk. Some cells are empty. The app crashed during two walks, and people who book by phone are never asked the dog's size. Before the weekly report, each gap needs a choice: drop the row, fill the gap, or flag it.

2. Count the gaps first

Here is the walk log. A missing value in a CSV is just an empty field, like the three empty fields after Kai on line 4.

walker,minutes,miles,dog_size Sam,30,1.2,small Lee,45,2.0,large Kai,,, Ana,25,1.0, Sam,40,1.6,medium Lee,,, Kai,35,1.4, Ana,50,2.2,large

Predict: after loading walks.csv, which column shows the most blanks?

import pandas as pd
walks = pd.read_csv("walks.csv")
walks.isna().sum()
walker      0
minutes     2
miles       2
dog_size    4
dtype: int64

Count before you decide anything. Then ask two questions about each gap. Why is it missing: was the value never collected, or was it lost? Where is it missing: is it spread out at random, or bunched in one group? The dog_size gaps are all phone bookings, so they are bunched in one group.

.isna() only counts empty cells. A 0 or the word "unknown" typed in place of a real value is missing too, but .isna() does not count it. Look at the rows as well as the counts.

3. Drop: when the row has nothing left

Kai's and Lee's crashed rows are blank in minutes, miles and dog_size. The 2 blanks in minutes and the 2 in miles are on the same two rows. Those rows have nothing to work with, so we drop them: remove the whole row.

New in this cell: walks.dropna(subset=["minutes"]) removes only the rows where minutes is empty. subset= names the column that decides the drop.

Predict: what does cell [2] print?

kept = walks.dropna(subset=["minutes"])
print(walks.shape)
print(kept.shape)
(8, 4)
(6, 4)

Without subset=, dropna() removes every row with any blank at all. That throws away Ana's and Kai's real walks just because the size is unknown:

walks.dropna().shape
(4, 4)

"I dropped the nulls" is not a reason. A real reason names the rows, says why they were empty, and gives a number: "I dropped 2 rows where the app crashed. Both had no minutes, miles or size, so nothing usable was lost."

4. Flag: when a guess would not be honest

Two of the walks in kept have no dog size. One option is to fill them with the mode, the size that shows up most often.

New in this cell: .mode() gives back a Series, because a column can have more than one most-common value. [0] takes the first one.

kept["dog_size"].mode()[0]
'large'

The known sizes are small, large, medium and large. "Large" wins with only 2 of 4, so writing "large" into the blanks would just be a guess. Instead, we flag them: leave the value missing and add a True/False column that marks it.

kept["size_unknown"] = kept["dog_size"].isna()
kept["size_unknown"].sum()
np.int64(2)

np.int64(2) is just the number 2. pandas shows the number's type around it. Each True counts as 1 when you add them up.

The flag keeps the report honest. Later you can give results with and without the walks whose size is unknown. Adding the column to kept does not change walks, because pandas 3 treats what dropna hands back as a separate table.

5. Fill: pick the stand-in that fits

Lee's phone lost its signal on two of eight days in a row, so minutes is blank on days 3 and 7. Day 5 was a 95-minute hike. Lee's other walks run 40 to 45 minutes. Here you can fill the gaps and compare two stand-ins side by side.

New in this cell: .fillna(value) puts value into every blank in a column and leaves the real values alone. Saving the result as a new column keeps the original minutes so you can compare.

Predict: which new column puts a number into days 3 and 7 that is longer than almost every real walk?

lee = pd.DataFrame({
    "day": [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8],
    "minutes": [40, 45, None, 42, 95, 44, None, 41],
})
lee["by_mean"] = lee["minutes"].fillna(lee["minutes"].mean()).round(1)
lee["by_median"] = lee["minutes"].fillna(lee["minutes"].median())
lee
   day  minutes  by_mean  by_median
0    1     40.0     40.0       40.0
1    2     45.0     45.0       45.0
2    3      NaN     51.2       43.0
3    4     42.0     42.0       42.0
4    5     95.0     95.0       95.0
5    6     44.0     44.0       44.0
6    7      NaN     51.2       43.0
7    8     41.0     41.0       41.0

The hike pulls the mean up to 51.2, longer than 5 of Lee's 6 real walks. The median, 43.0, sits right among them. The days are also in order, so a third choice is a forward-fill: copy the value from the day before.

New in this cell: .ffill() copies the value above into each blank, which is a forward-fill.

  1. Find day 7 in the table above. Look at the day just before it.
  2. Type the number by_previous will show for day 7. Type only the number, such as 40.
  3. Press Check, then run cell [7].

Why: knowing exactly which value a fill copies tells you whether it makes sense for your data.

lee["by_previous"] = lee["minutes"].ffill()
lee[["day", "minutes", "by_previous"]]
   day  minutes  by_previous
0    1     40.0         40.0
1    2     45.0         45.0
2    3      NaN         45.0
3    4     42.0         42.0
4    5     95.0         95.0
5    6     44.0         44.0
6    7      NaN         44.0
7    8     41.0         41.0

A backward-fill works the other way: .bfill() copies the value below each blank, from the day after. Here it would give day 3 the 42 from day 4, and day 7 the 41 from day 8. Backward-fill helps when the very first row is blank, because a forward-fill has no earlier day to copy from.

Use this table to pick a fill:

Fill withFits best when
MeanThe values are roughly balanced, with no outliers.
MedianThe data is skewed or has real outliers, like Lee's 95-minute hike.
ModeThe column holds categories, or one value clearly repeats the most.
Forward-fillThe rows are in order, and each value is close to the one before it.
Backward-fillThe rows are in order, and the first row (or a stretch at the start) is blank.

Fill and flag can work together. Before you fill, save lee["minutes"].isna() as a flag column. Then a reader can see that days 3 and 7 were filled in, not measured.

7. Wrap-up

  • Count the gaps with .isna().sum(), then ask why each one is missing and whether it is bunched in one group.
  • Drop a row only when it has nothing to work with, and use subset= so real values survive.
  • Fill when a fair stand-in exists (median for skewed data). Flag when a fill would be a guess. Say which rows, why, and how many.

New situation: next week, 3 of 40 walks are missing miles because a phone lost its GPS. Their minutes are fine. Most walks are 1 to 2 miles, but one hike was 9 miles. What is the best plan?

In interviews, "How do you decide whether to drop, fill, or flag missing data?" is a common question. A strong answer names the rows, the reason, and a number.
