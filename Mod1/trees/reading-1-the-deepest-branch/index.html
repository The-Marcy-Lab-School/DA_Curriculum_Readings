1. Concept overview

Picture a company's org chart. One person sits at the top with no one above them. That person has a couple of people reporting directly to them, and each of those people might have a couple of people reporting to them, and so on, branching outward and downward. You already have words for pieces of this shape without ever having taken a computer science class: the person at the top, the people who report to someone, the people who don't manage anyone at all, and two people who share the same manager.

Rename those everyday words and you already know most of the vocabulary of a tree: the CEO is the root, the one position with nothing above it. VP Engineering and VP Sales are children of the CEO, and the CEO is their parent. VP Engineering and VP Sales are siblings of each other, since they share a parent. The Engineering Manager and Sales Manager manage no one, so they're leaves: the ends of their branches.

A tree, formally, is built out of nodes. Each node holds a value (a name, a number, whatever the tree is storing) plus references to its children. That's the entire structure: no separate "tree object" holding a master list, just nodes pointing to other nodes, starting from one root.

An org chart doesn't limit how many people can report to one manager. But one very useful special case does limit that: a binary tree is a tree in which every node has at most two children, conventionally called its left child and its right child. Think of a customer-support chatbot that only ever asks yes-or-no questions: "is your issue about billing?" Yes leads one direction, no leads the other, and each of those questions can branch into one more yes/no question below it. Two branches per question, at most, is exactly what makes it a binary tree.

Why this matters: "walk me through how you'd find the depth of this tree" or "trace this tree traversal for me" are two of the most common data-structure questions in an entry-level technical interview, specifically because they're quick to draw on a whiteboard and reveal instantly whether a candidate can reason about a structure that isn't a flat list. Outside of interviews, real systems are full of tree shapes: a file system, a webpage's structure, a decision flow.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to write a function and call it, and you're about to lean on one extra idea: a function that calls itself, known as recursion, briefly re-explained below exactly where it's first needed. Recursion pairs well with trees specifically because a tree is built out of smaller trees: each child is the root of its own smaller tree, so "handle this whole tree" often reduces to "handle this one node, then let the same logic handle its children." If you've explored recursion as its own topic already, that habit of thought will feel very familiar here.

6. Visiting every node: traversal

Sometimes you don't need to find one specific node. You need to visit every node, in some predictable order, the way a file-search tool has to look inside every folder to find a file. That's a traversal.

One common order is called pre-order: visit the current node first, then fully traverse its entire left side, then fully traverse its entire right side. Notice the recursive shape of that description: "traverse the left side" means doing this exact same process again, starting from the left child instead of the root. That's recursion in action: the same rule, visit-then-go-left-then-go-right, applies at every single node, all the way down to the leaves, which just have no children left to traverse into.

7. The deepest branch: finding a tree's maximum depth

Here's one of the most common tree questions asked in technical interviews, and it's a great one to start with because it's almost entirely visual: given a binary tree, how many levels deep does it go? This is usually called the tree's depth (or sometimes height) — the number of levels counted from the root down to its single farthest leaf. A tree with only one node, just a root and nothing else, has a depth of 1.

Let's use the project folder from section 5 again, since it's already a tree you understand: project/ contains src/ and README.md; src/ contains utils/; utils/ contains helpers.py.

Predict first, before any counting: just by looking at the picture in section 5, how many levels deep is the deepest branch of this folder tree?

Level by level, the plain way: start at the root and count one level per step down the longest branch, ignoring shorter branches until you're sure you've found the longest one. Click through it below.

Now the way code actually computes it (recursion, re-explained): a function can't "look at the picture." It only ever knows about one node at a time, plus whatever its children's own results were. So the rule becomes recursive: the depth of a node is 1 (for itself) plus whichever of its two children reports back the bigger depth. A node with no child on one side just treats that missing side as depth 0. The recursion bottoms out at the leaves, which have no children at all, so both sides come back as 0 and the leaf reports depth 1.

def max_depth(node): if node is None: return 0 left = max_depth(node.left) right = max_depth(node.right) return 1 + max(left, right)

Bottom-up, the way the recursion actually resolves: the deepest node's result comes back first, and each parent combines its children's results as they return. Click through it below on the same folder tree.

10. How this shows up in interviews

"Find the maximum depth of a binary tree" is one of the single most common first tree questions asked in technical screens, precisely because it's practiced in this reading: it's visual, it has a clean recursive solution, and it lets an interviewer see whether you can reason about a structure instead of just reciting a definition. Being able to say out loud, "a node's depth is 1 plus the bigger of its two children's depths, and a missing child counts as 0," is exactly the kind of explanation interviewers are listening for.

Traversal questions show up just as often: "print every node in this tree" or "does this tree contain this value?" both start from the same pre-order idea practiced in section 6, visit the node, then its left side, then its right side.

A very common follow-up: "what if the tree only has a left child at every level, no right children at all?" That's a valid binary tree (a node can have zero, one, or two children), and it's worth noticing out loud that its depth equals its total number of nodes, since it's a single unbroken branch. Recognizing that edge case, unprompted, is a strong signal.

Interview-style question: a binary tree has one node at the root, with a left child, which has a left child, which has a left child (no right children anywhere). What is this tree's maximum depth?

12. Summary & critical thinking

A handful of ideas carried this whole reading: a tree is nodes pointing to children, starting from one root; a binary tree caps that at two children per node; a traversal visits every node in a defined order; and a tree's maximum depth is 1 plus the deeper of its two children's depths, all the way down to leaves reporting a depth of 1.

New situation: a family tree app stores each person as a node with references to their children (people can have more than two kids, so this is a general tree, not strictly binary). One branch traces back 5 generations from the person at the root; every other branch stops after 2 generations. What is this tree's maximum depth?

13. Thinking ahead

This reading stuck to binary trees and one traversal order, but the same root-and-children shape scales up in a few directions worth knowing about: a node can be allowed more than two children (a general tree, like the family-tree example above, or the org chart from section 1 before it was narrowed to binary), the values themselves can be kept in a sorted arrangement to make searching fast (a binary search tree), and once connections are allowed to point anywhere rather than strictly downward, the structure becomes a graph, which trees are technically a special case of. Recursion is worth deepening separately too. It's the exact same tool that made the traversal and the depth calculation in this reading work, and it comes up constantly once code needs to repeat a process on smaller and smaller pieces of the same structure.
