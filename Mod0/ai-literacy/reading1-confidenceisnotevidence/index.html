Confidence Is Not Evidence

A nonprofit's year-end funder report is due in two days. The program coordinator asks an AI assistant to summarize a semester of mentoring session logs, and it answers in three seconds: a specific percentage, a confident tone, even a citation to a study. Nothing about how that answer sounds tells you whether it is true. This reading is about building the habit that actually finds out.

1. Concept overview

Picture Bright Path, a small youth mentoring nonprofit. Its year-end funder report is due in two days, and the program coordinator types one question into an AI assistant: "Summarize how mentees did this semester." The assistant answers instantly: "87% of mentees showed improved grades this semester, consistent with a 2023 evaluation study of youth mentoring outcomes." It is specific, confident, and reads like it could be pasted straight into the report.

Nobody has opened the actual session log yet. That sentence is an AI-generated claim, not a verified fact — a claim is only confirmed once it is checked against something real, and checking is a step that happens outside the AI's answer, never inside it.

The Bright Path assistant's "87%" claim was delivered in exactly the same confident tone no matter which of these four boxes it actually belongs in. That is the whole problem with using tone as evidence: confidence and accuracy are two completely separate things, and an AI's wording never tells you which one you're looking at. Only checking the claim against the real session log can.

Why this matters: a number like this doesn't just sit in a chat window. If it lands in a funder report unchecked, an organization's credibility, and its next round of funding, rests on a sentence nobody confirmed. This is also one of the fastest-growing interview topics in data and software roles: employers now routinely ask candidates how they use AI tools and, more importantly, when they double-check them.

Where you're coming from, where this is headed: you already know how to tell structured data (rows and columns you can check directly) apart from unstructured text (a paragraph, a chat reply). This reading applies that same instinct to a new kind of unstructured text: an AI's answer. Once verifying one claim is automatic, the same habit scales to checking AI-suggested code, AI-suggested terminal commands, and AI-drafted summaries of your own real data.

6. Order the steps: how to verify an AI's claim

Click steps, in order, to build the sequence you'd follow to check a claim like Bright Path's "87%" statistic. Click a step again to remove it if you change your mind, then press "Check my order" when ready — this is a self-check, not graded on correctness.

  • Identify the exact factual claim being made: the number, quote, or citation.
  • Locate an independent, checkable source: the real dataset, the original document, or the org's own record.
  • Compare the AI's claim to what that independent source actually shows.
  • Decide whether to use the claim as-is, revise it, or discard it.

10. How this shows up in interviews

Interviewers increasingly ask directly about your relationship with AI tools: not whether you use them, but whether you know when to double-check them. "Tell me about a time an AI tool gave you something wrong, and how you caught it" is a real, increasingly common interview question in both data and software engineering interviews — the specific tool matters far less than showing you have a repeatable verification habit, not just good luck.

For data roles, being handed an AI-generated summary of a dataset and asked "how would you confirm this before it goes in front of a client or funder" is a realistic interview prompt. The skill being tested is exactly the read-the-real-source habit practiced in this reading, not any particular tool.

For software engineering roles, expect a version aimed at code: an interviewer hands you AI-generated code and asks what you'd check before merging it, tests, edge cases, whether it does what the comment claims, the same trace-before-trust habit from the terminal activity above, just applied to code instead of a command.

Interview-style: an AI code assistant writes a function for you and confidently states it handles empty input correctly. What's your first move?

12. Summary & critical thinking

Two habits carried this whole reading: treating any AI output as an unverified claim until it's checked against something real, and reading what an AI-suggested command actually does before ever running it.

New situation: a small clinic's front-desk AI assistant tells a staff member, "This patient's insurance is inactive, so reschedule the appointment." Nobody has opened the real insurance system yet. What should happen first?

13. Thinking ahead

Treating a confident-sounding answer as a claim until it's checked comes up again anywhere AI gets used for a real decision: reviewing AI-suggested code before merging it, checking an AI-drafted data summary against the actual numbers, and evaluating an AI-suggested fix to a script before ever running it.
