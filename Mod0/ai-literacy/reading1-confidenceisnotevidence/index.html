Confidence Is Not Evidence

An AI assistant gives you a polished answer in three seconds. Your challenge is not to decide whether AI is “good” or “bad.” It is to decide what deserves trust, what needs checking, and what you should never run blindly.

About 16–18 minNo videoSaves locallyImmediate feedback
Prep goal: arrive ready to distinguish a claim from a verified fact and use a double-check habit.

1. The three-second decision

A teammate asks an AI assistant, “How many records are in our customer file?” The assistant replies: “There are exactly 2,418 records.” It gives no source and shows no calculation.

What do you know right now?

Habit to build: fluent wording and confident tone are not evidence. Treat factual AI output as something to evaluate, not something automatically proven.

4. Risk radar: what could go wrong?

A project partner uses AI to draft background notes. The response contains a precise percentage and a professional-looking report title. Neither has a link.

Which response shows the strongest verification habit?

Two concrete risks to notice

Risk 1: a wrong statistic can be repeated as if it were true. Risk 2: a report, quotation, or citation can look plausible but not exist. The important move is to name the specific failure—not merely say “AI can be wrong.”

5. Command gate: read → predict → decide

AI coding help creates a second kind of trust decision: a command can affect real files. This simulator does not touch your computer. Use it to practice reading first.

$ practice-shell [Output]: Sandbox ready. Choose a command below to inspect its effect.

The simulator intentionally uses different filenames and examples from the upcoming lecture.

6. Build your double-check rule

A useful rule is concrete enough to change your behavior. If you are stuck, use the idea generator, then rewrite the suggestion in your own words.

7. Required bridge to lecture + lab

You just practiced the distinction between an AI-generated claim and a verified fact, identified concrete output risks, and used a read → predict → decide habit for AI-suggested commands. In lecture, you will deepen those ideas and practice verification with real command-line evidence. In the later project work, you will need to name concrete AI-output risks and state your own double-check rule.

New situation: During lab, an AI assistant says, “This CSV has no header row, so rename the columns now.” You have not opened the file yet. What should your first move be?

Be ready to recognize: claims that need external verification, sources you can actually inspect, and commands whose effects you should understand before running them. Those habits prepare you for the upcoming column-typing lab and project workflow.
